## Six words

> **Earn complexity; protect the test.**

*Glossa:* scelto via `narratore-critico` su $5$ varianti. Mantiene la struttura a due clausole con punto e virgola della serie (01 *"Programs follow rules; learners find them."*, 02 *"Draw the line; count the cost."*, 03 *"Same data; three algorithms, three boundaries."*, 04 *"Decisions cost data; hold the test."*, 05 *"Stack logistic units; descent finds weights."*, 06 *"Match the structure; share the weights."*, 07 *"Every token attends every; pre-train, fine-tune."*, 08 *"Structure the ask; check the answer."*, 09 *"Check the answer; gate the act."*). **Per il terzo capitolo consecutivo dopo il pivot di ch. 08, le due clausole sono *imperative* e il loro soggetto implicito è il clinico** — la serie ha pivotato da *cosa fa il modello* a *cosa fa lo studente*, e ch. $10$ chiude il corso in quella stessa voce. **"Earn complexity"** è Obj. $1$ nella sua formulazione *normativa esatta*: il testo dell'obiettivo recita *"each added unit of complexity must be **earned** by a failure of the simpler model"* — il verbo *earn* è già nel target, e il six-word lo eleva a *signature word*. Codifica due cose in tre parole: (a) la regola del *KISS + stop-criterion ex ante* (non parti grande, parti semplice e *guadagni* il diritto a salire) e (b) il framing *normativo* (la complessità è un **debito**, non un default). **"Protect the test"** è Obj. $2$ R2 nella sua forma operativa più dura: il test set è **sacro** — "*any decision after having seen the test = leakage*". *Protect* (non *hold*, non *seal*) è scelto perché evoca *vigilanza attiva* contro le pressioni del lab — *"guarda questo numero!"*, *"fai un'ultima prova!"* — che sono il meccanismo concreto attraverso cui il test si contamina nel mondo reale. Eco esplicito a ch. $04$ *"hold the test"*: ch. $04$ ha **introdotto** la regola (separazione train/val/test); ch. $10$ la **promuove** da *recipe* a *discipline difesa contro la tentazione*. Il *protect* di ch. $10$ è la versione *agonistica* dello *hold* di ch. $04$ — sei tu contro il tuo lab. Obj. $3$ (interpretability + clinical defensibility) **non** sta nel six-word: è importante operativamente, ma è una *scelta clinica contestualizzata*, non una *disciplina universale come earn/protect*; vive in pieno nella prosa e nell'arco. Anti-pattern evitati: *"Discipline outlasts the algorithm"* (vero, ma è il *payoff* della prosa, non il *meccanismo*), *"Start simple, defend clinically"* (poco affilato: *start* è meno normativo di *earn*), *"Keep it simple, stupid"* (slogan extra-serie, non appartiene a un corso clinico), *"Three disciplines outlast every algorithm"* (corretto ma declarativo invece di imperativo, rompe la serie post-pivot).

## Chapter arc — Hook → Challenge → Resolution → Payoff

| Punto | Funzione | Contenuto |
|---|---|---|
| **Hook** | aggancio operativo | Friday morning. La settimana di Elena è chiusa: lunedì il triage di $200$ lettere ED con il prompt strutturato (ch. $8$); mercoledì il workflow agente con il gate piazzato esattamente prima di step $4$ (ch. $9$). Il senior arriva con il prossimo audit: $30$-day ICU readmission, $5\,000$ pazienti, $50$ feature candidate, prevalenza $\sim 5\%$, da difendere a tre settimane di fronte al *clinical lead* e al *comitato etico*. Elena ha *ogni* strumento del corso. Ma sente, per la prima volta, che gli strumenti **non bastano**. Quello che le serve adesso non è un'altra tecnica — è una **disciplina di progetto**. |
| **Challenge / Data** | problema concreto | Tre trappole, nello stesso scenario, ciascuna delle quali farebbe fallire la defence al comitato. **(i) *Cominciare grande*.** Partire da un random forest perché "vince sempre" produce un modello inerpretabile *per ragioni che non sono cliniche* — e quando il comitato chiederà *"perché *questo* modello?"*, l'unica risposta onesta sarà *"perché ho scelto quello complicato per default"*. Indefendibile. **(ii) *Misurare male — in due modi*.** R1, *accuracy trap*: su $p = 5\%$ il classificatore "sempre-no" raggiunge $95\%$ di accuracy e $0$ di recall — *clinicamente inutile, statisticamente brillante*; e accuracy è la metrica che il $90\%$ dei poster clinici riporta. R2, *leakage rule*: **qualunque** decisione (hyperparameter, threshold, feature subset, model family) presa *dopo* aver visto un numero sul test trasforma quel test in un training set — *anche se il file si chiama "test.csv"*. È la regola più violata nella ricerca applicata, e il modo in cui lab seri producono paper irriproducibili. **(iii) *Scegliere l'interpretabilità per ragioni tecniche*.** Dire *"uso logistic perché è interpretabile"* senza chiedersi *quanti pazienti in più si sbagliano con la logistic* è scegliere per *abitudine*, non per *responsabilità clinica*. Il comitato etico chiede *clinical defensibility*; *technical defensibility* non basta. |
| **Resolution** | come si affronta | **(i) KISS + stop-criterion *ex ante*.** Prima riga del notebook, *prima del training*: *baseline = logistic regression su $5$–$10$ feature scelte clinicamente, AUPRC target $\ge 0.30$ (o equivalente: recall $\ge 0.60$ at precision $\ge 0.30$), escalate solo se il baseline fallisce sul validation set*. Lo stop-criterion va scritto *prima*: scritto *dopo* è razionalizzazione, non disciplina. La complessità si **guadagna** per fallimento del semplice — *each added unit of complexity must be earned by a failure of the simpler model*. **(ii) Metrica per il problema che hai, e test *protetto*.** R1: scarta accuracy su sbilanciati, scegli AUPRC / F1 / recall-at-precision e fissa la soglia *basandoti sul costo clinico relativo* di FP e FN (un readmission mancato costa diversamente da un'allerta falsa, e il costo è una scelta *clinica*, non *statistica*). R2: il test si guarda *esattamente una volta, alla fine, e mai più*. *Qualunque* decisione presa dopo aver visto un numero sul test = leakage — non importa quanto innocente sembri il *bump della regolarizzazione, just to check*. Lo stratagemma operativo: dedica un **fresh validation split** al thresholding e al tuning di iperparametri; il test resta sigillato. **(iii) Interpretabilità *matched* al modello, tradeoff *clinico*.** La tecnica segue la famiglia del modello: linear/logistic → coefficienti (log-odds per unità di feature, con segno e magnitudine); decision tree → decision path (sequenza di if-then leggibili); RF/GB → variable importance + SHAP per case-level; NN → SHAP o surrogate models. Ma la *scelta* fra *"logistic interpretabile e cieca"* e *"GB + SHAP opaca ma accurata"* **non è una scelta tecnica — è una scelta clinica**: se il SHAP-su-forest cattura abbastanza readmission da giustificare la perdita di interpretabilità built-in, allora il paziente è meglio servito dal *box con SHAP* che dal *semplice cieco*. Il comitato etico vuole il *clinical defensibility argument*, non il *technical one*. |
| **Payoff** | cosa resta | Il corso ha consegnato tre cose negli ultimi $15$ minuti: *parti semplice, misura onestamente, difendi clinicamente*. Non sono regole d'arte: sono **ordini di operazioni**. Senza la prima, qualunque modello complicato è una scommessa non motivata. Senza la seconda, qualunque numero su qualunque slide è dubbio (e *molti* numeri *sono* dubbi, nella letteratura applicata). Senza la terza, qualunque sistema "che funziona" è indifendibile al primo paziente che chiede *perché lei mi ha dimesso/non dimesso*. Sul $2 \times 2$ degli *AI users* — *Dependent Outsider* (no strumenti, no consapevolezza) / *Exposed Charlatan* (strumenti, no disciplina) / *Resistant Craftsman* (consapevolezza, no strumenti) / *Modern Alchemist* (strumenti **e** disciplina) — la **disciplina è la dimensione che separa l'*Exposed Charlatan* dal *Modern Alchemist***. Il corso ha consegnato gli strumenti; la disciplina è la parte che il corso *non può* consegnare. Si costruisce **progetto per progetto, audit per audit, defence per defence**. Elena non è uscita dal corso come *Modern Alchemist*; è uscita sapendo *cosa fa* uno, e sapendo che il quadrante successivo è una *pratica*, non un *titolo*. |

### Narrative version (prose)

Friday morning. The week that had begun on the Monday after the lecture on chapter $7$, with $200$ ED letters and a prompt that needed to be rewritten three times, and had passed through the Wednesday afternoon of chapter $9$ — the four-step agent, the PI's premature email, the gate placed exactly between *draft* and *send* — closed on a Friday in which Elena and her senior met in the small office at the end of the corridor. The senior put down a folder. *"Next audit. $30$-day ICU readmission. Five thousand patients on the hospital's de-identified extract, fifty candidate features, prevalence around five percent. The clinical lead wants something operational by the end of the month, and the ethics committee will see it three weeks from today. Build the model. Build it carefully."*

Elena had every tool the course had handed her. She knew how to frame the problem as $T/P/E$ from chapter $1$. She knew the difference between classification and regression, and which loss to use, from chapter $2$. She had three families of algorithms in her head from chapter $3$, a discipline for splitting the data and resisting overfitting from chapter $4$, the inner machinery of deep networks from chapter $5$, the constraints that made CNNs and RNNs tractable from chapter $6$, the architecture of the Transformer from chapter $7$, the three-piece kit for using a chat-LLM well from chapter $8$, and the trust-boundary discipline for agents from chapter $9$. She had, in other words, **eight chapters' worth of tools**.

And she sensed — sitting at her desk, the folder open in front of her — that the tools, by themselves, were not enough. *The tools were not the bit that would fail in front of the ethics committee.* What would fail was a sequence of *small wrong moves*, each defensible in isolation, accumulating into a model she could not justify when a clinician asked *why this one, why this number, why should I trust it on my next patient*. Those three questions were exactly the ones the senior had said the committee would ask. They were also the three questions that this final chapter — the chapter that began that Friday morning in her head, while the audit folder lay open — was about to answer.

She wrote three lines at the top of a fresh notebook.

> **Baseline: logistic regression on six clinically-selected features. Target: AUPRC $\ge 0.30$. Escalate to gradient boosting only if the baseline fails this target on the validation set.**

She read it twice and underlined *only if*. The thing she was doing, by writing this line *before* training a single model, was the thing the chapter had a name for: **earn the complexity**. If she'd written this line *after* — after a random forest had given her $0.34$, say, and she was now *defending* the random forest in her notes — it would have been a different sentence. It would have been a rationalisation, dressed up as a methodology, of a choice she had already made for unspoken reasons. *More parameters and richer features are paid for in robustness, interpretability, and risk of overfitting — so each added unit of complexity must be earned by a failure of the simpler model.* The simpler model deserved to be tried first, not because simple was *good*, but because complex was *expensive in defensibility*. Logistic regression on six features was the price of admission.

The six features came from clinical reasoning, not from automated selection. *Age. ICU length of stay. Charlson comorbidity index. Number of prior $90$-day admissions. Discharge-time estimated GFR. Number of medications at discharge.* Each one was something a clinical lead would *recognise* and *interrogate*; each one made sense without a SHAP plot to explain why the model was paying attention to it. If logistic regression on these six features hit AUPRC $\ge 0.30$, she would *ship that*, not the gradient boosting on $50$ features that would inevitably score higher. The committee would have a *defensible model* in their hands; her department would have an audit they could *operate*.

Then she made the second move. *Metrics.* On a dataset with $p = 5\%$, the world's worst classifier — the one that always says *no, this patient will not be readmitted* — would score $95\%$ accuracy on the held-out data, $100\%$ specificity, and $0\%$ recall. The numbers would look excellent in a poster abstract; the model would be *clinically useless*, because the entire clinical reason to build it was to *catch* the rare event. Accuracy was the metric for problems where both classes happened roughly equally often, and on $5\%$ prevalence it was just *wrong*. She picked AUPRC instead, with recall-at-precision as the secondary number reported alongside. AUPRC summarised the precision/recall curve in a single value that *did not get fooled* by the majority class; recall-at-precision was the operational number a clinician could read.

The threshold she would deliberately *not* set yet. The threshold was a *clinical decision* about the cost of false alarms relative to missed readmissions, and the cost ratio was a conversation she would have with the clinical lead the following week, not a number she would optimise on a curve. *A missed readmission costs a re-admission and a possible adverse event. A false alarm costs a follow-up call to a stable patient.* Those were not equivalent costs; the ratio was the clinical lead's to set; the threshold was Elena's to pick, *given* the ratio, *after* the validation curve had been drawn.

And then she made the move that the chapter was prepared to insist on hardest. She created a directory called `data/`, with three sub-directories: `train/`, `validation/`, and `test/`. She copied the de-identified extract into the appropriate splits — $70\%/15\%/15\%$, stratified by the outcome — and *immediately* moved `test/` into a separate folder on a separate drive, gave it a date-stamped name, and wrote a sticky note on her monitor that said `test data: open exactly once, on $2026$-$06$-$26$`. The date was three weeks away, the day of the ethics committee defence. Until then she would build the model — choose features, tune regularisation, set the threshold, write the report — using *only* the training and validation splits. The test was *not* hers to look at, no matter how curious she was about how the model was doing on it. *Anything she decided after looking at the test — even something as small as nudging the L$2$ penalty up by a factor of two because the training accuracy looked a bit high — would turn the test into a training set.* The file would still be called `test.csv`; the *epistemic property* of being a held-out sample would be gone. The chapter had a name for this discipline too: **protect the test**. *Looked at exactly once, at the end, and never again.*

The two and a half weeks that followed went, on the technical side, as the senior had probably anticipated. The logistic regression on the six features hit AUPRC $0.27$ on validation — *under* the $0.30$ target Elena had written down. So the stop-criterion fired in the direction it was designed to fire: she escalated. Gradient boosting on the same six features hit $0.31$; gradient boosting on a broader set of $15$ features (still clinically motivated) hit $0.34$. She picked the $15$-feature gradient boosting model, set the threshold based on the cost ratio her clinical lead had given her on the Tuesday of the second week (*"a missed readmission costs us about three times what a false alarm costs"*, which translated to a threshold around precision $0.40$ at recall $0.55$ on the validation curve), wrote up the methodology, and on the morning of $2026$-$06$-$26$ she opened the sealed test folder for the first and only time. The numbers held. Slightly under validation, as a faithful test always is, but well within the band she had committed to defend.

She walked into the ethics committee that afternoon with the model and the methodology, and the questions came as she had been told they would.

*"Why this model?"* — Because the logistic baseline failed the pre-registered target by three percentage points of AUPRC. The complexity was earned by a documented failure of the simpler model, not by a preference for the complicated one. *"Why this metric?"* — Because $p = 5\%$ makes accuracy useless ($95\%$ accuracy is the score of doing nothing); AUPRC and recall-at-precision are the metrics that survive class imbalance, and the threshold was chosen to match the clinical cost ratio our service uses, not to optimise the number on the slide. *"Why should we trust it on the next patient?"* — Because the model was evaluated on a test set that was not used to make any decision in the development process, and because for every prediction the committee will see a SHAP attribution that names *which features pushed the score in which direction* — not because SHAP is more interpretable than coefficients, but because *this specific model* (gradient boosting) does not *have* coefficients, and SHAP is the technique that *matches* this family. *"Why not logistic regression?"* — Because the logistic regression model would have under-detected readmissions by a number large enough that a clinical lead, presented with the choice, would have judged the loss of interpretability *worth* the gain in detection. *That* was Elena's argument, and it was a *clinical* argument: a sentence about the *patient*, not about the *model*.

The committee approved.

She walked out, and on the way to her desk she thought about the slide her teacher had shown at the end of the course — the $2 \times 2$ that had stayed pinned to her mental sketchpad ever since. *Dependent Outsider* in one quadrant: no tools, no awareness, surviving on what others build. *Exposed Charlatan* in another: tools, no discipline, producing impressive-looking work that the first audit destroys. *Resistant Craftsman* in a third: awareness, no tools, useful but isolated. *Modern Alchemist* in the fourth: tools *and* discipline, building things that survive both the patient and the committee. The course had not made her a *Modern Alchemist* — no course can. The course had *taught her to recognise where she stood, and which quadrant she now had the means to move toward*. The disciplines of chapter $10$ — the stop-criterion before training, the test protected from being peeked at, the interpretability technique chosen as a clinical argument rather than a technical one — were the **bits of the quadrant the course could hand her**. The rest was the project, the audit, the next patient, the next defence. *That* part of the quadrant was the part the course could not give her. It was the part she would build, project by project, over the years of her career.

She closed the laptop and stayed at the desk for a moment. The red risk score that had opened the course, in the very first scene, was still there somewhere in her future — a *next* red flag on a *next* screen, in a *next* hospital, on a *next* patient she was about to discharge. By then she would know not only what the box was, but how to interrogate it, how to ground its answer in evidence, and how — when the evidence said so — to override it *in writing*, with reasons a colleague could read and a committee could approve. The course had ended. The practice was about to begin.
