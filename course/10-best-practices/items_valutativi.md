> **Set size note:** chapter 10 closes at **5 items**, one more than [chapter 9](../09-agents/items_valutativi.md) and the *applicativa*-only chapters. The choice is deliberate: cap. 10 has **three objectives**, but **Obj. 2 contains two independent rules** — *R1 — accuracy trap* (on imbalanced data, accuracy is uninterpretable because it cannot distinguish the model from always-predict-majority) and *R2 — leakage rule* (any decision taken after observing a performance number on a dataset converts that dataset into a training set). The two rules cannot share an item: R1 is a *metric-choice* discipline (which number to report), R2 is a *test-discipline-over-time* rule (when the test stops being a test). They fail in *different scenarios*, expose *different misconceptions*, and require *different operational fixes*. Testing them in a single MCQ would force the student to choose *which one to apply*; testing them in two MCQs measures both, separately, on their own diagnostic distractors. Plus the **synthesis item** (Item 05 — critique a colleague's project plan that violates all four rules in cascade) — same role as Item 04 of cap. 09: detect the *integrated misapplication* in a design the student did not write, which is the operational form of *all three disciplines together*. Three objectives × {Obj.1 + Obj.2.R1 + Obj.2.R2 + Obj.3 + synthesis} = $5$ items, each at its natural cognitive level.

> **Note on numbers and scenarios:** all five items anchor the same clinical scenario as the chapter's summative exercise (*Monday's audit, Friday's defence* — $30$-day ICU readmission, $5\,000$ patients, $50$ candidate features, prevalence $p \approx 5\%$, defence to clinical lead + ethics committee in three weeks — see [objectives.md](objectives.md)). The anchor is *deliberate continuity*: the chapter teaches **three disciplines on one project**, not three disciplines on three abstract problems, so the items reuse the project scenario to keep the *target of the disciplines* constant. **Operational variation across items** prevents mechanical repetition: each item gives the project to a different colleague (Marco for Item 01, Sara for Item 02, Davide for Item 03, Anna for Item 05 — the names anchor *who is making the mistake*), with different feature counts ($6$ / $8$ / $6$ / $\sim 12$ / $50$), different metric reportings ($AUPRC \ge 0.30$ target / $94.8\%$ accuracy / sequential $0.29 \to 0.31$ on test / $0.32$ vs $0.27$ baseline-comparison / $\ge 90\%$ threshold), and different stages of the project (planning / lab-meeting reporting / methodology-sequence narration / post-audit defence writing / Friday-morning plan email). The items measure the *rule and its operational test*, never the *recall of the summative's specific planning-sheet rows*.

## Coverage

**By objective** (all three chapter objectives covered, with Obj. 2 testing both rules separately, plus a synthesis item that exercises all three together):

- **Obj. 1** (earn complexity, KISS + stop-criterion ex ante): item 01 (Comprehension, MCQ) covers it directly on the *timing* axis; item 05 (Argumentation) exercises it as the *recognition that Anna has skipped the baseline and reached for a population-prior justification* (*"random forest is the best class for tabular clinical data"*).
- **Obj. 2 R1** (accuracy trap): item 02 (Comprehension/Application, MCQ) covers it directly on the *baseline-against-which-accuracy-must-be-compared* axis; item 05 (Argumentation) exercises it as the *recognition that Anna's $\ge 90\%$ threshold sits below the $1 - p = 95\%$ baseline* — the lethal cascade.
- **Obj. 2 R2** (leakage rule): item 03 (Application, MCQ) covers it directly on the *decision-after-observation* axis; item 05 (Argumentation) exercises it as the *recognition that combining train+test for cross-validation is leakage by construction*.
- **Obj. 3** (match interpretability + clinical defensibility): item 04 (Application, applicativa) covers it directly on both components (matching rule + clinical framing); item 05 (Argumentation) exercises it as the *recognition that coefficients-on-random-forest is a mismatch* AND that *"if the committee asks"* is an *afterthought*, not a clinical defensibility argument.

**By cognitive category** (three of four categories present — multi-categorial, like cap. 9):

- Knowledge: $0$
- Comprehension: $1$ (item 01); plus *Comprehension/Application* mixed: $1$ (item 02)
- Application: $2$ (items 03 + 04)
- Argumentation/evaluation: $1$ (item 05)

Cap. 10 is **multi-categorial**, like cap. 9 and unlike the *applicativa-only* chapters (cap. 8). The reason is the chapter's structure: cap. 10 introduces *three disciplines* (Obj. 1 + Obj. 2 + Obj. 3), each at its natural cognitive level — *recognise the rule's binding form* (Comprehension, for Obj. 1's *ex ante* timing), *apply the rule's metric or decision test* (Comprehension/Application + Application, for Obj. 2's R1 and R2), *apply the matching + frame the tradeoff* (Application, for Obj. 3) — and the *integrated misapplication* on someone else's design (Argumentation, for the synthesis). Flattening all five items to Application would *lose* the discrimination between *seeing when the rule binds* (Item 01 — the *timing of the stop-criterion is the discipline*) and *applying the rule when triggered* (Items 03 + 04). The chapter teaches three *operational disciplines*, and the item bank registers each at its natural cognitive level.

## Internal coherence — the five items together test the chapter's three disciplines as one project's lifecycle

The chapter's narrative closes on a single project: *Friday morning — the audit folder lands on Elena's desk, three weeks to defend, $5\,000$ patients, $50$ features, prevalence $5\%$.* The five items map onto the five operational moves Elena makes between Friday and the defence:

- **Item 01** (Marco's planning document) tests the *first move* — *before any training*, write the baseline + the target + the escalation rule, and bind the team to it. A student who confuses *written-ex-post-as-rationalisation* with *written-ex-ante-as-discipline* has not understood what *earn complexity* asks for. The item measures the *first thing Elena does*.
- **Item 02** (Sara's lab-meeting result) tests the *second move* — *when a colleague reports a metric*, ask whether the metric can distinguish the model from the trivial baseline. A student who is impressed by $94.8\%$ accuracy on $p = 5\%$ has missed the *baseline-against-which-accuracy-must-be-compared* discipline. The item measures *the question Elena asks of someone else's numbers*.
- **Item 03** (Davide's five-step sequence) tests the *third move* — *during model development, recognise the moment the test stops being a test*. A student who sees Davide's L$2$-bump-after-test as *honest reporting if both numbers are shown* has confused *disclosure* with *correctness*. The item measures *the discipline Elena imposes on her own workflow*.
- **Item 04** (writing the defence document) tests the *fourth move* — *given the model has been chosen, write the interpretability paragraph that the clinical lead can read aloud back to the ethics committee*. A student who writes *"SHAP because it's the standard"* has produced *terminology*, not a *matching argument*; a student who writes *"interpretability is always better"* has *bypassed the tradeoff* rather than *posed it*. The item measures *the discipline Elena applies in the defence document*.
- **Item 05** (Anna's mis-conceived plan) tests the *fifth move* — *spotting the integrated misapplication of all three disciplines on someone else's project plan*. This is the operational *meta-skill* the chapter is ultimately for. A colleague has almost-but-not-quite reached for the disciplines; the student must see the *four violations* and *propose a corrected plan that composes the three disciplines as an order of operations*. The item measures *the generalisation of Elena's discipline beyond her own audit*.

Together, the five items verify the chapter's *discipline* rather than its *vocabulary*. A student who passes all five has demonstrated: *how to plan* (item 01), *how to read someone else's metric* (item 02), *how to protect their own test* (item 03), *how to defend their model clinically* (item 04), and *how to critique a colleague's plan when all three disciplines are violated at once* (item 05). The chapter's summative exercise — *Monday's audit, Friday's defence*, with its three-section planning sheet — is anchored as *the same scenario* across all items, but **the items measure the rule, not the summative's specific row-fillings**.

## Anti-leakage — vocabulary boundary with cap. 01–09

The items stay strictly within cap. 10 vocabulary: *baseline*, *logistic regression*, *random forest*, *stop-criterion (ex ante)*, *AUPRC*, *recall-at-precision*, *F$1$*, *prevalence* ($p$), *always-predict-NO* / *always-predict-majority*, *train/validation/test split*, *held-out test*, *fresh validation split*, *sealed test*, *leakage*, *interpretability (built-in vs post-hoc)*, *coefficients*, *decision path*, *variable importance*, *clinical defensibility*, *clinical lead*, *ethics committee*, *audit*, *cost ratio*, *order of operations*, *earn complexity*, *protect the test*, *accuracy trap*, *match interpretability*.

They do **not** presuppose any concept from earlier chapters that has not become part of the cap. 10 register: no *KNN* / *decision boundary* (cap. 02–03), no *CNN* / *RNN* / *pre-training* (cap. 05–06), no *Transformer* / *attention* / *encoder-decoder* (cap. 07), no *prompt-slot* / *$7$-slot template* / *competence rule* (cap. 08), no *agent* / *control loop* / *interaction mode* / *trust boundary* / *gate* / *reversibility test* / *Slack* / *Teams* / *webhook* / *HL7 alert* / *PDF overwrite* / *do-stuff mode* (cap. 09). The single shared term across chapters is *clinical defensibility*, which is introduced in cap. 10 as the proper framing of the interpretability tradeoff (Obj. 3) — and is not used in cap. 09 in the same operational sense (cap. 09 uses *trust-boundary discipline*, not *clinical defensibility*).

**Two specific anti-leakage choices**, both relating to the chapter's narrative:

1. *SHAP* is mentioned in the chapter's [narrative](narrative.md) (Elena uses *"SHAP on a forest"* in the defence) and listed in [objectives.md](objectives.md) as the technique for *neural networks*. The official mapping for *RF / GB* in Obj. 3 is **variable importance**, not SHAP. The items therefore use *variable importance* as the *required* technique for random forest; *SHAP / LIME / Shapley values* are accepted as **bonus** at Excellent on Item 04 and Item 05 *if* the citation is anchored operationally (the student names what SHAP *adds* over variable importance — per-patient attribution that the global ranking does not provide). The items do **not** require the student to have done independent reading on SHAP.

2. *Gradient boosting* is mentioned in the chapter's [narrative](narrative.md) as the model Elena escalates to (*"SHAP on a forest, because the simple one missed too many"*) and listed in Obj. 3's mapping (*RF / gradient boosting* $\to$ *variable importance*). The items use **random forest** as the escalation model rather than gradient boosting, because random forest is the model the class has actually seen demonstrated in earlier chapters. Item 05 (Anna's plan) uses random forest deliberately to keep the *escalation target* familiar to the student; Anna's mismatch (*coefficients on random forest*) is testable without relying on independent knowledge of gradient boosting's specific structure.
