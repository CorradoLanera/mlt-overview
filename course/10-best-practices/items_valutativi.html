Set size note: chapter 10 closes at 5 items, one more than chapter 9 and the applicativa-only chapters. The choice is deliberate: cap. 10 has three objectives, but Obj. 2 contains two independent rules — R1 — accuracy trap (on imbalanced data, accuracy is uninterpretable because it cannot distinguish the model from always-predict-majority) and R2 — leakage rule (any decision taken after observing a performance number on a dataset converts that dataset into a training set). The two rules cannot share an item: R1 is a metric-choice discipline (which number to report), R2 is a test-discipline-over-time rule (when the test stops being a test). They fail in different scenarios, expose different misconceptions, and require different operational fixes. Testing them in a single MCQ would force the student to choose which one to apply; testing them in two MCQs measures both, separately, on their own diagnostic distractors. Plus the synthesis item (Item 05 — critique a colleague's project plan that violates all four rules in cascade) — same role as Item 04 of cap. 09: detect the integrated misapplication in a design the student did not write, which is the operational form of all three disciplines together. Three objectives × {Obj.1 + Obj.2.R1 + Obj.2.R2 + Obj.3 + synthesis} = 55 items, each at its natural cognitive level.

Note on numbers and scenarios: all five items anchor the same clinical scenario as the chapter's summative exercise (Monday's audit, Friday's defence — 3030-day ICU readmission, 50005\,000 patients, 5050 candidate features, prevalence p≈5%p \approx 5\%, defence to clinical lead + ethics committee in three weeks — see objectives.md). The anchor is deliberate continuity: the chapter teaches three disciplines on one project, not three disciplines on three abstract problems, so the items reuse the project scenario to keep the target of the disciplines constant. Operational variation across items prevents mechanical repetition: each item gives the project to a different colleague (Marco for Item 01, Sara for Item 02, Davide for Item 03, Anna for Item 05 — the names anchor who is making the mistake), with different feature counts (66 / 88 / 66 / ∼12\sim 12 / 5050), different metric reportings (AUPRC≥0.30AUPRC \ge 0.30 target / 94.8%94.8\% accuracy / sequential 0.29→0.310.29 \to 0.31 on test / 0.320.32 vs 0.270.27 baseline-comparison / ≥90%\ge 90\% threshold), and different stages of the project (planning / lab-meeting reporting / methodology-sequence narration / post-audit defence writing / Friday-morning plan email). The items measure the rule and its operational test, never the recall of the summative's specific planning-sheet rows.

Internal coherence — the five items together test the chapter's three disciplines as one project's lifecycle

The chapter's narrative closes on a single project: Friday morning — the audit folder lands on Elena's desk, three weeks to defend, 50005\,000 patients, 5050 features, prevalence 5%5\%. The five items map onto the five operational moves Elena makes between Friday and the defence:

  • Item 01 (Marco's planning document) tests the first move — before any training, write the baseline + the target + the escalation rule, and bind the team to it. A student who confuses written-ex-post-as-rationalisation with written-ex-ante-as-discipline has not understood what earn complexity asks for. The item measures the first thing Elena does.
  • Item 02 (Sara's lab-meeting result) tests the second move — when a colleague reports a metric, ask whether the metric can distinguish the model from the trivial baseline. A student who is impressed by 94.8%94.8\% accuracy on p=5%p = 5\% has missed the baseline-against-which-accuracy-must-be-compared discipline. The item measures the question Elena asks of someone else's numbers.
  • Item 03 (Davide's five-step sequence) tests the third move — during model development, recognise the moment the test stops being a test. A student who sees Davide's L22-bump-after-test as honest reporting if both numbers are shown has confused disclosure with correctness. The item measures the discipline Elena imposes on her own workflow.
  • Item 04 (writing the defence document) tests the fourth move — given the model has been chosen, write the interpretability paragraph that the clinical lead can read aloud back to the ethics committee. A student who writes "SHAP because it's the standard" has produced terminology, not a matching argument; a student who writes "interpretability is always better" has bypassed the tradeoff rather than posed it. The item measures the discipline Elena applies in the defence document.
  • Item 05 (Anna's mis-conceived plan) tests the fifth move — spotting the integrated misapplication of all three disciplines on someone else's project plan. This is the operational meta-skill the chapter is ultimately for. A colleague has almost-but-not-quite reached for the disciplines; the student must see the four violations and propose a corrected plan that composes the three disciplines as an order of operations. The item measures the generalisation of Elena's discipline beyond her own audit.

Together, the five items verify the chapter's discipline rather than its vocabulary. A student who passes all five has demonstrated: how to plan (item 01), how to read someone else's metric (item 02), how to protect their own test (item 03), how to defend their model clinically (item 04), and how to critique a colleague's plan when all three disciplines are violated at once (item 05). The chapter's summative exercise — Monday's audit, Friday's defence, with its three-section planning sheet — is anchored as the same scenario across all items, but the items measure the rule, not the summative's specific row-fillings.

Anti-leakage — vocabulary boundary with cap. 01–09

The items stay strictly within cap. 10 vocabulary: baseline, logistic regression, random forest, stop-criterion (ex ante), AUPRC, recall-at-precision, F11, prevalence (pp), always-predict-NO / always-predict-majority, train/validation/test split, held-out test, fresh validation split, sealed test, leakage, interpretability (built-in vs post-hoc), coefficients, decision path, variable importance, clinical defensibility, clinical lead, ethics committee, audit, cost ratio, order of operations, earn complexity, protect the test, accuracy trap, match interpretability.

They do not presuppose any concept from earlier chapters that has not become part of the cap. 10 register: no KNN / decision boundary (cap. 02–03), no CNN / RNN / pre-training (cap. 05–06), no Transformer / attention / encoder-decoder (cap. 07), no prompt-slot / 77-slot template / competence rule (cap. 08), no agent / control loop / interaction mode / trust boundary / gate / reversibility test / Slack / Teams / webhook / HL7 alert / PDF overwrite / do-stuff mode (cap. 09). The single shared term across chapters is clinical defensibility, which is introduced in cap. 10 as the proper framing of the interpretability tradeoff (Obj. 3) — and is not used in cap. 09 in the same operational sense (cap. 09 uses trust-boundary discipline, not clinical defensibility).

Two specific anti-leakage choices, both relating to the chapter's narrative:

  1. SHAP is mentioned in the chapter's narrative (Elena uses "SHAP on a forest" in the defence) and listed in objectives.md as the technique for neural networks. The official mapping for RF / GB in Obj. 3 is variable importance, not SHAP. The items therefore use variable importance as the required technique for random forest; SHAP / LIME / Shapley values are accepted as bonus at Excellent on Item 04 and Item 05 if the citation is anchored operationally (the student names what SHAP adds over variable importance — per-patient attribution that the global ranking does not provide). The items do not require the student to have done independent reading on SHAP.

  2. Gradient boosting is mentioned in the chapter's narrative as the model Elena escalates to ("SHAP on a forest, because the simple one missed too many") and listed in Obj. 3's mapping (RF / gradient boosting →\to variable importance). The items use random forest as the escalation model rather than gradient boosting, because random forest is the model the class has actually seen demonstrated in earlier chapters. Item 05 (Anna's plan) uses random forest deliberately to keep the escalation target familiar to the student; Anna's mismatch (coefficients on random forest) is testable without relying on independent knowledge of gradient boosting's specific structure.
