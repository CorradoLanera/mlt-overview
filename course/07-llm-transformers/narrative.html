Six words

Every token attends every; pre-train, fine-tune.

Glossa: scelto via narratore-critico su 5 varianti. Mantiene la struttura a due clausole con punto e virgola della serie (01 "Programs follow rules; learners find them.", 02 "Draw the line; count the cost.", 03 "Same data; three algorithms, three boundaries.", 04 "Decisions cost data; hold the test.", 05 "Stack logistic units; descent finds weights.", 06 "Match the structure; share the weights.") — la coerenza tipografica è ormai cifra della serie e va rispettata. "Every token attends every" è il primo dei due beat del capitolo: attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(QK⊤dk)V\text{attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\dfrac{Q K^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V in una frase di inglese clinico — parallel, direct access da ogni posizione a ogni altra, senza il loop ricorrente del ch. 06. "Pre-train, fine-tune" è il secondo beat: l'economia che rende un LLM usabile per un clinico — pre-training massivo una sola volta (∼$108\sim \$10^8), fine-tuning su corpus task-specific molto più cheap (∼$104\sim \$10^4), un'asimmetria di ordine 10410^4. Lessico-perno attend (radice di attention) seminato per le slide e per la Sentence S del sommativo; pre-train / fine-tune seminati per la mini-griglia. Eco esplicito a ch. 06 "share the weights" → ch. 07 "attends every": in entrambi la mossa è strutturale; ma qui per la prima volta la regola di ripetizione del ch. 06 non viene rifinita — viene abbandonata. Niente self-attention, Q/K/V, encoder/decoder, autoregressive nel six-word — vocabolario tecnico riservato all'arc e alla prosa. Anti-pattern evitati: "Transformer beats RNN" (il capitolo non è una gara), "Attention is all you need" (slogan extra-serie), "GPT is just a Transformer" (cancellerebbe i beat di P e G).

100 words (3 acts)

The famous box of Elena's career arrived as a chat window: people typed, it answered. She had heard it called a Transformer, but did not know what that meant. So she read the acronym backwards. T — every token saw every other token in parallel, no serial loop, no propagation through hidden states. P — the box had already swallowed the statistics of language before her query. G — it sampled the next token autoregressively, one at a time. Chat was the wrapper. Three engineering choices and one UI choice. Only the rule of repetition had changed; this time it had dropped entirely.

Chapter arc — Hook → Challenge → Resolution → Payoff

Punto Funzione Contenuto
Hook aggancio culturale ChatGPT — il manufatto ML più nominato della carriera di Elena, e l'unico che lei abbia sentito chiamare da pazienti, parenti, giornali. Sa che dietro la chat c'è un Transformer; non sa che cosa significhi. Il capitolo non è "spiegare come funziona davvero un Transformer" (in 25′25' non si fa) — è smitizzare la scatola: nominarla nei suoi pezzi e dire perché ha sostituito le RNN del ch. 06.
Challenge / Data problema concreto Una frase clinica corta — "The patient denies a history of penicillin allergy, however the chart from 20192019 that the resident finally located this morning explicitly contradicts him." — contiene una dipendenza a lungo raggio: per rispondere "è allergico?" il modello deve collegare denies (posizione iniziale) e contradicts (posizione finale). La RNN del ch. 06 propaga a<t>=g(Waaa<t−1>+Waxx<t>+ba)a^{<t>} = g(W_{aa} a^{<t-1>} + W_{ax} x^{<t>} + b_a) posizione per posizione — la dipendenza tra posizione 11 e posizione TxT_x deve attraversare Tx−1T_x - 1 stati intermedi, ad ogni passo c'è una occasione di perdere il segnale. Tre domande in una: (i) c'è un meccanismo che salti il loop? (ii) come si chiama la scatola costruita attorno a quel meccanismo? (iii) perché un clinico (non un ingegnere ML) può ragionevolmente usarla lunedì mattina?
Resolution come si affronta (i) Self-attention. A ogni posizione, la rappresentazione del token è calcolata come combinazione pesata di ogni altro token dell'input, in parallelo: attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(QK⊤dk)V\text{attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\dfrac{Q K^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V, dove ad ogni token si associano una query QQ, una key KK e un value VV. La dipendenza tra posizione 11 e posizione TxT_x è ora una sola operazione di distanza — la matrice QK⊤Q K^{\top} è Tx×TxT_x \times T_x e mette in relazione tutte le coppie di posizioni in un colpo. È matrix multiplication, che le GPU calcolano massivamente in parallelo — il loop sequenziale della RNN non era solo lento, era non parallelizzabile. (ii) Transformer. È l'architettura del paper "Attention is all you need" (20172017), costruita attorno a stack di self-attention (più, in modelli encoder/decoder, cross-attention). Chat-G-P-T, decodificato all'indietro: T = Transformer; P = Pre-trained (la rete ha già imparato un modello statistico generale del linguaggio leggendo un corpus enorme, prima di vedere qualunque task dell'utente); G = Generative (a inference il modello produce il token successivo campionando da p(token∣prompt)p(\text{token} \mid \text{prompt}) e ri-feeda quel token come input — autoregressivo, un token alla volta); Chat = il wrapper multi-turn che mantiene la conversazione come parte del prompt. (iii) Transfer learning. Pre-trained ha un significato operativo: un singolo pre-training costoso (∼$108\sim \$10^8 per i modelli di frontiera) produce un modello che già sa la statistica generale del linguaggio; un fine-tuning molto più cheap (∼$104\sim \$10^4 su un corpus task-specific, p. es. lettere di dimissione + ICD coding) lo adatta a un compito specifico, con ordini di grandezza meno dati e meno compute. L'asimmetria ∼104×\sim 10^4 \times è ciò che rende realistica un'applicazione biomedica di un LLM — un dipartimento universitario non pre-trena un LLM, ma può ragionevolmente fine-tunarlo.
Payoff cosa resta ChatGPT è tre scelte ingegneristiche in un wrapper UI — Transformer, Pre-training, Generative, in un'interfaccia Chat. Non è una nuova specie di computazione, non è una scatola sigillata; è un'architettura nominabile (QK⊤Q K^{\top} è scrivibile in una riga) e un'economia nominabile (pre-train caro, fine-tune cheap). La sostituzione delle RNN non è solo qualità della rappresentazione: è parallelismo, e quindi scalabilità sulla GPU — la ragione per cui 20172017 ha cambiato di scala il campo. Elena non sa ancora come parlare al modello (ch. 08), non sa ancora quando l'autonomia diventa pericolosa (ch. 09); ma sa cos'è la cosa con cui sta per parlare. L'opacità di scala del ch. 05 e di struttura del ch. 06 è ora completata dall'opacità di corpus: la scatola è stata plasmata da un qualcosa che Elena non ha letto. Quell'opacità non sparirà — diventerà la base operativa della regola di competenza del ch. 08.

Narrative version (prose)

Of every box Elena had met that year — the classifier of chapter 22, the algorithms of chapter 33, the cross-validation of chapter 44, the deep network of chapter 55, the CNNs and RNNs of chapter 66 — this was the one her patients named. Her uncle had said the word at dinner; the discharge nurse had typed into it that morning to draft a letter; the trauma resident had asked it for a differential and shown her the screen with half-pride and half-suspicion. ChatGPT. The most famous box of her career, and the one she had never opened. She had heard it called a Transformer; what that word meant on the inside, no one she trusted had ever explained to her.

She began with the kind of sentence the model would actually have to read:

"The patient denies a history of penicillin allergy, however the chart from 20192019 that the resident finally located this morning explicitly contradicts him."

To answer "is the patient allergic to penicillin?" the box had to connect denies, early in the sentence, with contradicts, late in the sentence — a dependency that spanned the sentence. The recurrent network of chapter 66 would have walked through that sentence position by position, updating its hidden state a<t>=g(Waaa<t−1>+Waxx<t>+ba)a^{<t>} = g(W_{aa}\, a^{<t-1>} + W_{ax}\, x^{<t>} + b_a) one step at a time, carrying the trace of denies through every intervening word — through the chart, through the year, through the resident, through "this morning" — until it reached contradicts. The signal had ample opportunity to fade. And the loop was serial: position tt could not even be computed until position t−1t-1 had been. A modern GPU's parallelism stood idle.

The Transformer, she read, did the opposite. At every position, the representation of the token was computed as a weighted combination of every other token in the input, in parallel, in one shot:

attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(QK⊤dk)V.\text{attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\dfrac{Q K^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V.

Each token was projected into three vectors — a query QQ, a key KK, and a value VV. The matrix product QK⊤Q K^{\top} was a Tx×TxT_x \times T_x table of all pairs of positions at once, the softmax normalised it into weights, and the value matrix VV recombined the tokens according to those weights. The dependency between denies and contradicts was no longer a path of Tx−1T_x - 1 intermediate states; it was a single entry in QK⊤Q K^{\top} — one operation away. That move had a second consequence Elena had not initially seen: it turned sequence modelling into a problem of matrix multiplication, which a GPU could parallelise across thousands of cores. The RNN had been slow not because it was clever, but because it was serial. The Transformer was fast because it had abandoned that constraint. By the late 20202020s it had replaced the RNN in nearly every sequence task that mattered.

That was the T. Three more letters to go.

P was Pre-trained. The box she was about to type into had not been built for her query. It had been built — months earlier, on cluster GPUs that drew the electricity of a small town — by being shown a generic corpus of text the size of, roughly, every published book and a substantial fraction of the public web, and asked, billions of times, to predict the next token. By the time the training run ended, the parameters had absorbed an enormous statistical model of how language is structured: which words follow which, how sentences are built, what answers questions of which form. The pre-training did not yet know any task — but it had digested the substrate every task is written on.

G was Generative. At inference, the model did not return a class label or a regression value the way the classifiers of chapter 22 did. It returned a token — and then another, and then another. Each token was sampled from a conditional distribution p(token∣prompt)p(\text{token} \mid \text{prompt}) that the pre-training had taught it; the sampled token was appended to the input, and the model was asked again, until it produced an end-of-sequence signal or the user stopped it. Autoregressive. That was why the answer arrived one word at a time on her screen.

Chat was the simplest of the four: a multi-turn dialogue wrapper. The conversation history was kept in the prompt; the model was made to play the assistant role. A UI choice, not an architectural one.

Three engineering choices, one interface choice. Chat-G-P-T, in reverse: a Transformer (T), pre-trained on a massive corpus (P), running autoregressively to generate text (G), wrapped in a chat protocol. The famous box of Elena's career had been named.

What was left was the question every clinician would, sooner or later, want to ask: can my group actually use one of these? The answer was the deepest part of the chapter, and it had a name: transfer learning. A pre-training run on the open web cost on the order of 10810^8 dollars; a fine-tuning run that took the same pre-trained model and adapted it to a specific task — say, summarising discharge letters, or coding diagnoses to ICD-10 — cost, on a credible biomedical corpus of a few thousand examples, on the order of 10410^4 dollars. Four orders of magnitude. The pre-training was not something Elena's department would do; the fine-tuning was. The economy was what made the architecture appropriable. Without it, ChatGPT would have been a curiosity for the labs that could afford to build their own; with it, the same box could land on a clinical research budget.

By the end of the afternoon, the famous box was no longer famous, and no longer a box. It was a Transformer — a stack of self-attention layers — pre-trained on a corpus Elena had not seen and could not audit, generative one token at a time, chatted through a UI choice. It had replaced the RNN not because attention was philosophically deeper than recurrence, but because QK⊤Q K^{\top} ran on a GPU in a way that a<t>=g(Waaa<t−1>+…)a^{<t>} = g(W_{aa} a^{<t-1>} + \dots) never would. The opacity of scale (chapter 55) and the opacity of structure (chapter 66) were now joined by a new one: the opacity of corpus. The box had been shaped by something Elena had not read. She did not yet know what to do with that fact. She was about to find out.

Pre-hook → 08 Using ChatGPT in practice

Elena knew what ChatGPT was, but she had not yet typed a single word into it. The Monday after the lecture, 200200 anonymised ED discharge letters were waiting on her shared drive — trauma vs non-trauma, for an audit her senior wanted by Friday. She opened a chat window. She typed "classify these as trauma or non-trauma". The answer was unusable. She rewrote. The answer was less unusable but still wrong. By the third attempt she was rewriting the prompt itself, not the question — and she realised, with the small annoyance of a discipline she had not been taught, that structure was a thing one could put inside a prompt, the way one puts structure inside a SQL query or a study protocol. And: was chat even the right channel for 200200 letters, or did she need a different way to deliver the same instruction at volume? And, most uncomfortably: which of these classifications would she be unable to check on her own — and therefore, by the simplest possible argument, unable to delegate in the first place? The next chapter answers exactly those three questions: a structured prompt, a rule for what to delegate and what to keep, and a channel matched to the size of the job. The box of chapter 77 has not changed. The question has: from what is this thing to what do I do with it on Monday.
