Six words

Structure the ask; check the answer.

Glossa: scelto via narratore-critico su 5 varianti. Mantiene la struttura a due clausole con punto e virgola della serie (01 "Programs follow rules; learners find them.", 02 "Draw the line; count the cost.", 03 "Same data; three algorithms, three boundaries.", 04 "Decisions cost data; hold the test.", 05 "Stack logistic units; descent finds weights.", 06 "Match the structure; share the weights.", 07 "Every token attends every; pre-train, fine-tune."). Per la prima volta nella serie, le due clausole sono entrambe imperative e — più importante — il loro soggetto implicito non è la scatola, è il clinico. Sette capitoli di seguito hanno descritto cosa fa il modello (six-word con soggetto: "programs", "learners", "units", "tokens"); ch. 08 è il primo che descrive cosa fa lo studente (six-word con verbo all'imperativo rivolto al clinico). È il pivot d'asse pedagogico del corso, codificato nella tipografia del six-word. "Structure the ask" è Obj. 1: il 7-slot template (role / context / task / instructions / output / style / examples) — qui sintetizzato in struttura un'interrogazione invece di sparare una domanda. Ask è scelto sotto-tecnico (non prompt, non query) perché il pubblico è Elena lunedì mattina, non un ML engineer. "Check the answer" è Obj. 2, la regola della competenza, in forma operativa: l'unica cosa che permette di delegare è la capacità di verificare indipendentemente. Check è il verbo-chiave, e — non a caso — è esattamente il verbo che ch. 09 metterà a rischio (l'agente agisce prima che tu checki); il check di ch. 08 è la cosa che ch. 09 minaccerà. Obj. 3 (Web / Prompt design / API + token cost) non sta nel six-word: è importante operativamente ma non è il cuore pedagogico del capitolo (è una decisione tattica, non un'etica), e i six-word della serie hanno sempre privilegiato i due beat strutturali sopra gli altri. Eco esplicito a ch. 06 e ch. 07: Match the structure (ch. 06, struttura del dato) → Structure the ask (ch. 08, struttura della domanda) — la parola structure attraversa il corso lungo assi diversi (architettura, dato, prompt); è un signature word della serie. Anti-pattern evitati: "Prompt engineering" (jargon), "Use AI carefully" (generico), "Web, prompt, API" (descrittivo invece di prescrittivo), "Don't trust ChatGPT" (sbaglia il payoff, che è trust calibrato, non sfiducia).

Chapter arc — Hook → Challenge → Resolution → Payoff

Punto Funzione Contenuto
Hook aggancio operativo Lunedì mattina, dopo la lezione del ch. 07. Elena conosce cosa è ChatGPT — Transformer, Pre-trained, Generative, in Chat. Non sa ancora come usarlo. Sul drive condiviso del suo gruppo trovano 200200 lettere di dimissione ED anonimizzate; il senior chiede una triage trauma vs non-trauma per un audit di qualità, entro venerdì. Apre chat.openai.com, digita "classify these as trauma or non-trauma". La risposta è inutilizzabile. Sapere cos'è la scatola non era sapere come parlarci.
Challenge / Data problema concreto Tre domande operative che il capitolo deve risolvere in 15′15': (i) Come si struttura una richiesta in modo che il modello produca l'output che serve invece di una risposta generica? Una one-liner non basta; serve un'architettura della domanda. (ii) Quando non si delega? Alcune lettere hanno fraseggio non standard (es. una fragility fall in un'9292-yo descritta in linguaggio geriatrico, dove la regola clinica "caduta da fragilità = trauma" non è evidente dal lessico). Se Elena non sapesse catturare l'errore del modello su quelle lettere, delegare sarebbe delegare l'ignoranza. (iii) Su quale canale? Web (chat.openai.com) ha senso per una domanda; prompt design in chat ha senso per una manciata; ma per 200200 lettere copy-paste si rompe — serve programmabilità. Quale soglia? E con quale unità di costo?
Resolution come si affronta (i) 7-slot prompt. Una richiesta robusta ha sette pezzi nominati: role (chi impersona il modello), context (la situazione del task), task (l'unico verbo da eseguire), instructions (la procedura), output (la forma esatta del deliverable — "return only one token: 'trauma' or 'non-trauma'"), style (vincoli su tono, formato, e su cosa NON aggiungere), examples (1–3 coppie I/O lavorate). Ogni slot sopprime un failure mode specifico: assenza di role → tono indeciso; assenza di output → il modello spiega invece di classificare; assenza di examples → drift sui casi al margine. (ii) Regola della competenza. Delegare solo task il cui risultato sai giudicare indipendentemente. Operativamente: per ogni candidato-task identifica (a) un failure mode plausibile (citazione inventata, dosaggio arrotondato a un numero verosimile, linea-guida allucinata); (b) la competenza che ti serve per catturarlo. Se non hai la competenza (b), non delegare. La regola è normativa, non opzionale. (iii) Web / Prompt design / API + token. Il token è l'unità: 11 token ≈34\approx \dfrac{3}{4} word (i.e. ∼750\sim 750 parole ≈1000\approx 1\,000 token, ∼750000\sim 750\,000 parole ≈106\approx 10^6 token). Per 200×300=60000200 \times 300 = 60\,000 parole di input, 60000×43=8000060\,000 \times \dfrac{4}{3} = 80\,000 token, più ∼200\sim 200 token di output (un label per lettera): ∼80000\sim 80\,000 token totali. Regola operativa di canale: Web per la one-shot ad-hoc; Prompt design (in chat) per una manciata di task strutturati fatti a mano; API per qualunque workflow che debba essere ripetibile, integrato in altro software, o applicato a volume (≳100\gtrsim 100 items). Per le 200200 lettere la risposta è API.
Payoff cosa resta ChatGPT è operativamente accessibile lunedì mattina, ma solo dopo tre discipline: una struttura della richiesta (7 slot, ogni slot sopprime un errore noto), una regola di onestà rispetto alla propria competenza (non delego ciò che non saprei controllare), un canale dimensionato sul volume del compito (web →\to prompt design →\to API, con il token come unità di costo). La scatola del ch. 07 non è cambiata; è cambiato dove Elena si posiziona rispetto a essa: non più davanti a chiedersi cos'è, ma accanto a usarla — e a non usarla, nei casi giusti. Il payoff del capitolo non è "ora sai prompt engineering"; è "non confondere delegare bene con delegare tutto". Quella distinzione — delegating well vs delegating everything — è il livello minimo di pragmatismo che la spine del corso aveva promesso ("trusting it where the evidence holds, overriding it where it doesn't"). Per la prima volta nel corso, Elena non ha imparato un meccanismo; ha imparato una disciplina.

Narrative version (prose)

Monday after the lecture. The slides of chapter 77 had named the parts of ChatGPT — Transformer, Pre-trained, Generative, Chat — and Elena had walked out feeling, for the first time in months, that the box at the centre of every conversation was a nameable object. By Monday morning the box had ceased to be a curiosity. On the shared drive of her research group, 200200 anonymised emergency-department discharge letters had been waiting for her since Friday, with a one-line request from her senior at the top of the folder: "Triage these into trauma vs non-trauma by Friday for the QI audit." Each letter ran to about three hundred words; some were neatly templated, some were typed by tired residents at three in the morning. She opened chat.openai.com, pasted the first letter, and wrote her question the way a clinician asks a colleague over coffee:

"Is this trauma or non-trauma?"

The model replied with a paragraph that was almost helpful. It explained that trauma could refer to physical injury, psychological distress, or — in the context of the letter — a fall, and that the letter she had pasted appeared to describe a fall in an elderly patient but that further information would be needed to classify it definitively. She read the paragraph twice. It contained no error, and no answer. She tried again, slightly more crisply, and got a slightly more crisp non-answer. By the third attempt she had stopped rewriting the question and was rewriting the prompt itself. There was, she realised — and this was the small private discovery that the chapter would be built around — structure one could put inside a prompt.

She started over. Role: "You are a clinical-coding assistant for an internal hospital QI audit." Context: "You are reading anonymised emergency-department discharge letters from a teaching hospital; the audit is internal and not patient-facing." Task: "Classify each letter as trauma or non-trauma." That was the one verb; she resisted the temptation to chain it with "and explain why" — explaining was a different task. Instructions: "Read the chief complaint and the mechanism-of-injury fields; if either names an external traumatic event — fall, MVA, assault, fracture from external force — classify trauma." Output: "Return only one token: 'trauma' or 'non-trauma'. Do not return any other text, any explanation, any preamble." Style: "No reasoning shown, no qualifications, no caveats." Examples: two short worked cases, one trauma (a 3434-yo MVA), one non-trauma (a urinary tract infection). Seven slots, each one suppressing a specific failure mode the previous attempts had displayed: role killed the helpful-paragraph reflex; task killed the and-also-explain drift; output killed the qualified-non-answer; examples anchored the edge cases.

The next ten letters classified cleanly. Then she hit a letter that gave her pause. An 9292-year-old woman, brought in by family, with a fall at home described in a register Elena recognised as geriatric fragility framing — vague mechanism, no obvious external force, the kind of letter that names frailty and home environment before it names fall. The model returned non-trauma. Elena, who had read enough geriatric medicine to know that fragility fractures are trauma in any sensible audit, marked the answer wrong. She flagged the letter for manual review. So far, so good — the failure had been caught.

But then a quieter thing happened, and it was the part that would change how she thought about LLM use forever. The next letter described what seemed, at first read, an unremarkable urinary tract infection in a middle-aged man. The model returned non-trauma. Elena nodded and moved on. Then — three letters later, by chance — she went back and noticed a single phrase mid-letter she had not registered the first time: "presented after a workplace incident involving a chemical exposure". That was trauma, by the audit's own definition. The model had missed it. So had she, the first time. The model had been wrong in a way that looked right to a non-expert, and only the second reading by a clinician had caught it.

She put the API down — figuratively, since she had not yet moved to the API — and wrote down a sentence the chapter would be remembered for: "I should only delegate to this model tasks whose answer I can independently judge." For the two-letter geriatric fragility case she had used clinical knowledge; for the chemical-exposure case she had used a second careful reading of the letter. Both were competencies she possessed. For the letters in the corpus that turned on, say, an obscure psychiatric inclusion criterion — a competency she did not possess — the rule said no. Not "no, never use the model"; not "no, never delegate" — no, not this delegation, because I cannot check it. The rule was operational, not philosophical: if a plausible-looking failure mode of the model would slip past me, I am not the right person to delegate this task in the first place.

Then there was the matter of channel. Twenty letters in, the copy-paste-and-read rhythm of the chat was costing her about a minute per letter, and at that rate 200200 letters would consume more than three hours and produce no machine-readable artefact for the audit — only a screen full of "trauma" and "non-trauma" tokens she would have to transcribe by hand. She switched. The same prompt — the same seven slots, the same prohibition on explanation — was wrapped in an API call that read the letters from disk and wrote the labels to a CSV. The economy of the call was reckoned in tokens, not in words: 11 token ≈34\approx \dfrac{3}{4} word; 200200 letters times 300300 words per letter was 6000060\,000 words; 60000×43=8000060\,000 \times \dfrac{4}{3} = 80\,000 tokens of input, plus a negligible 200200 tokens of output. The whole audit ran for less than the price of a coffee, and produced exactly the artefact the senior had asked for.

What Elena had assembled, by lunchtime, was the chapter's three-piece kit. First, a way to ask — the seven slots, each one targeted at a failure mode she had seen herself. Second, a way to say no — the competence rule, in its operational form: delegate only what you can independently check. Third, a way to deliver — Web chat for the ad-hoc one-shot; Prompt design in chat for the small batch done by hand; API for the repeatable, integrated, volume workload; the token, not the word, as the unit of cost. Each piece on its own was easy. The discipline was in putting all three together, and in refusing to delegate the two cases on which the rule said no. Delegating well was not delegating everything. That was the chapter's payoff, and it was the first time in the course Elena had learned a practice rather than a mechanism.

By Friday afternoon the audit was on the senior's desk, with the two non-delegated cases manually flagged and reviewed.

Pre-hook → 09 Agents

For all of Monday morning, every output the model produced passed through Elena's eyes before it became anything. The label trauma did not reach the audit spreadsheet until she had read it. The non-delegated cases did not become decisions until she had reviewed them. Reading was the trust gate. Wednesday afternoon, her senior asked the same model to do four things in sequence: (1) fetch the latest delirium-screening guidelines from PubMed; (2) summarise their inclusion criteria into a one-page table; (3) draft a one-page audit protocol based on the summary; (4) email it to the PI. By the end of the fourth step, no human was scheduled to read between the model's reasoning and the email leaving. Something had been added to the box that had not been there on Monday: a way for it to look up the world (a search tool), a way for it to act on the world (a mail client), and a loop that decided, after each step, which of those to do next. The chat had become an agent. And the question Elena had been so careful with on Monday — which output do I check before it becomes anything? — had become spatial: where, exactly, in a four-step chain, does the gate go? Step 11 is reversible; step 44 is irreversible the moment the SMTP socket closes. The next chapter is exactly that placement: the trust boundary. The box has not changed, again. But now it can act.
