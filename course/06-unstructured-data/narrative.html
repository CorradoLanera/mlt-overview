Six words

Match the structure; share the weights.

Glossa: scelto via narratore-critico su 5 varianti. Mantiene la struttura a due clausole con punto e virgola della serie (01 "Programs follow rules; learners find them.", 02 "Draw the line; count the cost.", 03 "Same data; three algorithms, three boundaries.", 04 "Decisions cost data; hold the test.", 05 "Stack logistic units; descent finds weights.") — la coerenza tipografica è ormai cifra della serie e va rispettata. "Match the structure" è il primo dei due beat del capitolo: l'architettura non è una scelta libera, è dettata dalla forma del dato (spaziale → CNN, sequenziale → RNN) — è la riformulazione letterale di Obj. 1 ("Match an unstructured-data type to the right deep architecture"). "Share the weights" è il secondo beat: l'unico trucco ingegneristico che rende sia CNN sia RNN tractable — un kernel piccolo riusato su tutte le posizioni dell'immagine; le stesse matrici applicate a ogni passo della sequenza. Lessico-perno weights ripreso dal ch. 05 (lì "descent finds weights", qui "share the weights") — coerenza esplicita: i pesi non cambiano natura, cambia come vengono riusati. Niente kernel, convolutional, recurrent, attention nel six-word — vocabolario seminato solo nel pre-hook e nella prosa.

Chapter arc — Hook → Challenge → Resolution → Payoff

Punto Funzione Contenuto
Hook aggancio Elena's fully-connected machinery of chapter 5 was satisfying on a 1212-feature tabular row. Then the next case file lands on her desk: a chest X-ray at 1024×10241024 \times 1024 pixels. A fully-connected first layer with one hidden neuron would need over a million weights — more parameters in the first layer than her dataset has patients. The "deep" machinery did not break; it simply did not scale to data that looked like the real world.
Challenge / Data problema concreto Two unstructured-data types fail the MLP for two different structural reasons. (a) Spatial single-information (X-ray, CT slice, histology tile) — the same edge or lesion can appear anywhere in the field of view, and an MLP has no built-in reason to treat two displaced instances similarly: it must re-learn every translation from scratch. (b) Sequential single-information (ECG trace, clinical note, heart-rate stream) — the position tt within the sequence carries meaning, and an MLP fed the flat vector has no notion of before and after. One question: can a single engineering principle fix both?
Resolution come si affronta Yes — the same trick on two different axes: weight sharing. For images, a small kernel K∈ℝk×kK \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k} (typically 3×33 \times 3, so 99 trainable weights) is slid over the input; at every spatial position (i,j)(i, j) the feature map is Fij=g(∑u,vKuvXi+u−1,j+v−1)F_{ij} = g\!\left(\sum_{u, v} K_{uv} \, X_{i + u - 1,\, j + v - 1}\right). The same KK is reused everywhere — one operator across space — and the parameter count stops growing with image size. For sequences, at every position t=1,…,Txt = 1, \ldots, T_x the hidden state is updated by a<t>=g(Waaa<t−1>+Waxx<t>+ba)a^{<t>} = g\!\left(W_{aa}\, a^{<t-1>} + W_{ax}\, x^{<t>} + b_a\right), optionally emitting y<t>=g′(Wyaa<t>+by)y^{<t>} = g'\!\left(W_{ya}\, a^{<t>} + b_y\right). The same matrices Waa,Wax,WyaW_{aa}, W_{ax}, W_{ya} are reused at every tt — one update rule across time — and the parameter count stops growing with sequence length.
Payoff cosa resta What looked like two new technologies is one trick applied to two axes of the data. A 3×33 \times 3 convolutional kernel has 99 weights for the whole layer, regardless of image size — versus 5017650\,176 weights per output neuron for a fully-connected layer on a 224×224224 \times 224 grayscale input. An RNN with 55-dim input, 1010-dim hidden state, 22-dim output has 170170 weights, regardless of sequence length — versus ∼250000\sim 250\,000 for the analogous MLP on a 50005\,000-sample ECG. Three orders of magnitude fewer parameters in both cases — and the saving is paid for by encoding the data's structure into the architecture, rather than asking the network to discover that structure during training. CNN and RNN are not a new kind of network. They are MLPs constrained — fully-connected nets in which the weights are forced to repeat along the axis the data lives on. The black box of chapter 5 was big; now it is also smart, in a very specific sense: it knows what kind of data it is reading.

Narrative version (prose)

The first case file on Elena's desk that morning was a chest X-ray — 1024×10241024 \times 1024 grayscale pixels of a patient she would not see in person. The "deep network" of the previous chapter had been satisfying on tabular data: twelve features, twelve weights per neuron, four equations she could write on a napkin. But a fully-connected first layer over this image, even with a single hidden neuron, would already need over a million weights — and a network with ten hidden layers, on a dataset of a thousand patients, would have orders of magnitude more parameters than examples. She did not need a bigger model. She needed the same model, with a constraint.

The next case file was an ECG: 50005\,000 scalar samples at 500500 Hz, ten seconds of cardiac rhythm. Flatten that into a vector and feed it to an MLP, and the MLP has no notion that sample 49994\,999 comes after sample 11 — it sees five thousand columns of a spreadsheet, in whatever order they happen to be arranged. The order, on an ECG, is the signal. An MLP could not even describe atrial fibrillation, because atrial fibrillation is a pattern over time. The fully-connected machinery had been built to learn arbitrary functions of unstructured vectors; it had not been built to respect the structure of what was inside the vector.

Two data types, two reasons the same machinery failed. And — to her relief — one engineering response.

The trick was weight sharing. For the X-ray, define a small kernel — say a 3×33 \times 3 matrix KK of nine trainable weights — and slide it across the image, computing, at every position (i,j)(i, j), a feature map Fij=g(∑u,vKuvXi+u−1,j+v−1)F_{ij} = g\!\left(\sum_{u, v} K_{uv} \, X_{i + u - 1,\, j + v - 1}\right). The same nine weights were used at every position. There was one kernel for the entire image, not one per pixel. If KK had learned to detect an edge, it would detect that edge wherever the edge appeared — positional invariance was now part of the network's structure, not something the network had to discover during training. And the weight count stopped growing with image size: nine weights for the whole layer, full stop.

For the ECG, the analogous move was on the time axis. At every position t=1,…,Txt = 1, \ldots, T_x the network updated a hidden state a<t>=g(Waaa<t−1>+Waxx<t>+ba)a^{<t>} = g\!\left(W_{aa}\, a^{<t-1>} + W_{ax}\, x^{<t>} + b_a\right), optionally emitting an output y<t>=g′(Wyaa<t>+by)y^{<t>} = g'\!\left(W_{ya}\, a^{<t>} + b_y\right). The same three matrices Waa,Wax,WyaW_{aa}, W_{ax}, W_{ya} were used at every tt. There was one update rule for the whole sequence, applied recursively. The hidden state a<t>a^{<t>} depended on a<t−1>a^{<t - 1>}, which depended on a<t−2>a^{<t - 2>}, and so on — so a<t>a^{<t>} carried, at least in principle, the network's memory of everything it had read up to that point. The weight count stopped growing with sequence length: an RNN with five-dimensional input, a ten-dimensional hidden state and a two-dimensional output had 170170 weights to learn — independent of whether the ECG was ten seconds long or ten minutes.

The same trick, on two different axes. CNN: share across space. RNN: share across time. Both of them, mathematically, were fully-connected networks with constraints — the weights were forced to repeat along the axis the data was structured on. Strip the constraints and you got back the MLP. Keep them, and you had a model that could read a million-pixel image with nine weights, or a five-thousand-sample sequence with one hundred and seventy.

By the end of that afternoon Elena was no longer wondering why "deep learning" needed two architectures instead of one. She was wondering whether every data type would have its own architecture-and-sharing rule. Three-dimensional volumetric CT scans? Graphs of patient interactions in a ward? Probably yes, probably the same idea each time. The atoms — logistic units, weights, descent — had not changed at all. What had changed was the rule of repetition: how often, and along which axis, the same weights were going to be reused. That was what made an architecture an architecture.

Pre-hook → 07 LLMs & Transformers

The RNN was the canonical answer for sequences, but Elena could already feel the limit. Reading an ECG one sample at a time is fine: a<t>a^{<t>} depends on a<t−1>a^{<t-1>}, and the cardiac rhythm is a local pattern that the recurrence will catch in a few steps. But a discharge letter is harder: the meaning of "no" at position 4242 depends on "but" at position 55, and the network would have to propagate that dependency through 3737 intermediate hidden states — each one a chance for the signal to fade. Is there a way to let every position see every other position directly, without the sequential bottleneck? That is the question of the next chapter. The answer is attention, and the architecture built around it is called the Transformer — the network that replaced RNNs in nearly every sequence task by the late 20202020s and that, wrapped in a chat interface, became the most famous machine-learning artefact of Elena's career. The atoms, again, do not change. Only the rule of repetition does — one more time.
