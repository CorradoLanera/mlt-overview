Six words

Decisions cost data; hold the test.

Glossa: scelto via narratore-critico su 4 varianti. Mantiene la struttura a due clausole con punto e virgola dei capitoli precedenti (01 "Programs follow rules; learners find them.", 02 "Draw the line; count the cost.", 03 "Same data; three algorithms, three boundaries.") — la coerenza tipografica è ormai cifra della serie. Riprende esplicitamente il verbo cost del cap. 02 (count the cost) — lì era il costo della classificazione sbagliata (rischio), qui è il costo informazionale di ogni decisione presa su una stima. "Hold the test" è la regola operativa del capitolo: il test set è l'unico numero affidabile, e lo è proprio perché non è stato speso in nessuna scelta. La tagline pesa: ogni numero che usi per scegliere, lo stai consumando.

Chapter arc — Hook → Challenge → Resolution → Payoff

Punto Funzione Contenuto
Hook aggancio The "best" classifier from chapter 3 — the kk-NN with k=1k=1 that misclassified zero of Elena's training patients — meets its first new patient and gets it wrong. Then another. Then another.
Challenge / Data problema concreto Training error is the model's reflection of itself, not of the world. Worse, the more flexible a model becomes, the better it can fit any specific training set — at the cost of fitting that one set, rather than the underlying pattern (overfitting). She needs a way to tell, before she deploys, whether a classifier has learned the signal or only memorised the sample. And the question is double: among several candidate models, how does she choose? And once she has chosen, how does she report a trustworthy estimate of how well it will do on new patients?
Resolution come si affronta She splits her data three ways: a training set to fit each model's parameters; a validation set to choose between candidate models / hyperparameter settings; and a test set, held out, used only once at the very end to estimate the chosen model's performance. The validation step is operationalised by KK-fold cross-validation: divide the (non-test) data into KK equal blocks; for k=1,…,Kk = 1, \ldots, K train on the other K−1K - 1 blocks and estimate the error on block kk; average the KK error rates. Standard KK is in the range [5,10][5, 10]. The unifying rule is the contamination principle: every time you take a decision based on a number from a dataset, that number is no longer a valid estimate. Selection consumes the validation set; the test set is the only one that survives untouched.
Payoff cosa resta She can now tell apart a model that has learned the signal from one that has merely memorised the data — and can report a number (the test error) that has not been spent in any earlier decision. Looking back at the three boundaries of chapter 3, she can finally say which of them holds on new patients: the one whose validation error was lowest, with its honest test error attached. The cliff-hanger of chapter 2 ("but on a different group of patients?") and the trap of chapter 3 (11NN's paper-thin wrap) are both answered by the same procedure.

Narrative version (prose)

Elena had built three classifiers in chapter three — kk-NN, kernel SVM, Random Forest — and the simplest test, training error, had whispered an answer that felt too easy: the 11-NN had got every training patient exactly right. Before she trusted that number, she tried the model on a small group of patients she had quietly held aside, and the verdict was brutal — many of them landed wrong, and the supposedly best classifier had turned out to be the worst. Sitting with that, she saw what training error actually measures: how well a model fits the rows it has already seen, and nothing about the rows still to come. The more flexible she let the model be — the smaller kk, the fancier the decision boundary — the more perfectly it could redraw its training set, and the further it could drift from the world the training set was meant to represent. She needed a discipline. So she carved her data into three parts. One she kept to fit the model — the training set. One she used to choose between candidate models, by giving each a turn at being trained on most of the data and scored on the rest in turn — the validation set, organised as a KK-fold cross-validation (with KK commonly 55 or 1010: divide into KK equal blocks, train on K−1K-1, estimate the error on the held-out one, repeat, and average). The third part she touched only once, at the very end, when she had already chosen — the test set, held out, the source of the single trustworthy number she could quote back to a clinician. The reason there had to be three sets, not two, was a rule about information she had not seen named before: every time you take even a single decision based on a number on a dataset, that number is no longer a valid estimate. Choosing the model consumes the validation set; reporting performance has to be done on a set the choice never touched. Decisions had cost her data, and so she had paid in advance. By the end of that afternoon Elena could look back at the three boundaries from chapter three — wiggly, smooth, staircase — pick the one whose validation error was lowest, and report its test error as a number that meant what it said. The pushback she had heard from "Clinician A" — "but on a different group of patients?" — was no longer a hypothesis hanging in the air. She had the answer. And she had it because she had been willing, on day one, to set some data aside and not spend it.

Pre-hook → 05 Deep Learning

Elena now has a discipline that works for any classifier she can build — fit it, cross-validate it, hold out a test set, report the honest number. But what if the model itself can be made arbitrarily complex — a network of millions of artificial neurons that can, in principle, fit anything she shows it? The cross-validation discipline still holds. But the architecture of the model becomes a new question: how many layers, how many neurons, how do they connect? That class of models — deep neural networks — is next.
