## Six words

> **Decisions cost data; hold the test.**

*Glossa:* scelto via `narratore-critico` su 4 varianti. Mantiene la struttura a due clausole con punto e virgola dei capitoli precedenti (01 *"Programs follow rules; learners find them."*, 02 *"Draw the line; count the cost."*, 03 *"Same data; three algorithms, three boundaries."*) — la *coerenza tipografica* è ormai cifra della serie. Riprende esplicitamente il verbo *cost* del cap. 02 (*count the cost*) — lì era il costo della classificazione sbagliata (rischio), qui è il costo *informazionale* di ogni decisione presa su una stima. **"Hold the test"** è la regola operativa del capitolo: il test set è l'unico numero affidabile, e lo è proprio perché *non è stato speso* in nessuna scelta. La tagline pesa: ogni numero che usi per scegliere, lo stai consumando.

## 100 words (3 acts)

Elena saw $1$NN's perfect score and almost believed it. Then she remembered her own pushback from the second chapter — *on a different group of patients?* — and tested the model on a handful she had set aside. Many were wrong — the supposedly best was the worst. She learned what training error really measures: how a model fits the patients it has already seen, nothing about the ones to come. So she divided her data three ways: one to fit, one folded several times to choose between models, one untouched to confirm. Decisions had cost her data; she had paid in advance.

## Chapter arc — Hook → Challenge → Resolution → Payoff

| Punto | Funzione | Contenuto |
|---|---|---|
| **Hook** | aggancio | The "best" classifier from chapter 3 — the $k$-NN with $k=1$ that misclassified *zero* of Elena's training patients — meets its first new patient and gets it wrong. Then another. Then another. |
| **Challenge / Data** | problema concreto | Training error is the model's reflection of itself, not of the world. Worse, the *more flexible* a model becomes, the better it can fit any specific training set — at the cost of fitting that one set, rather than the underlying pattern (*overfitting*). She needs a way to tell, *before* she deploys, whether a classifier has learned the signal or only memorised the sample. And the question is double: among several candidate models, how does she **choose**? And once she has chosen, how does she report a *trustworthy* estimate of how well it will do on new patients? |
| **Resolution** | come si affronta | She splits her data three ways: a **training set** to fit each model's parameters; a **validation set** to choose between candidate models / hyperparameter settings; and a **test set**, *held out*, used only once at the very end to estimate the chosen model's performance. The validation step is operationalised by **$K$-fold cross-validation**: divide the (non-test) data into $K$ equal blocks; for $k = 1, \ldots, K$ train on the other $K - 1$ blocks and estimate the error on block $k$; average the $K$ error rates. Standard $K$ is in the range $[5, 10]$. The unifying rule is the **contamination principle**: *every time you take a decision based on a number from a dataset, that number is no longer a valid estimate*. Selection consumes the validation set; the test set is the only one that survives untouched. |
| **Payoff** | cosa resta | She can now tell apart a model that has learned the signal from one that has merely memorised the data — and can report a number (the test error) that has not been spent in any earlier decision. Looking back at the three boundaries of chapter 3, she can finally say *which* of them holds on new patients: the one whose validation error was lowest, with its honest test error attached. The cliff-hanger of chapter 2 ("but on a different group of patients?") and the trap of chapter 3 ($1$NN's paper-thin wrap) are both answered by *the same* procedure. |

### Narrative version (prose)

Elena had built three classifiers in chapter three — $k$-NN, kernel SVM, Random Forest — and the simplest test, *training error*, had whispered an answer that felt too easy: the $1$-NN had got every training patient exactly right. Before she trusted that number, she tried the model on a small group of patients she had quietly held aside, and the verdict was brutal — many of them landed wrong, and the *supposedly best* classifier had turned out to be the *worst*. Sitting with that, she saw what training error actually measures: how well a model fits the rows it has already seen, and nothing about the rows still to come. The more flexible she let the model be — the smaller $k$, the fancier the decision boundary — the more perfectly it could redraw its training set, and the further it could drift from the world the training set was meant to represent. She needed a discipline. So she carved her data into three parts. One she kept to *fit* the model — the **training set**. One she used to *choose* between candidate models, by giving each a turn at being trained on most of the data and scored on the rest in turn — the **validation set**, organised as a $K$-fold cross-validation (with $K$ commonly $5$ or $10$: divide into $K$ equal blocks, train on $K-1$, estimate the error on the held-out one, repeat, and average). The third part she touched *only once*, at the very end, when she had already chosen — the **test set**, held out, the source of the single trustworthy number she could quote back to a clinician. The reason there had to be three sets, not two, was a rule about information she had not seen named before: *every time you take even a single decision based on a number on a dataset, that number is no longer a valid estimate*. Choosing the model consumes the validation set; reporting performance has to be done on a set the choice never touched. Decisions had cost her data, and so she had paid in advance. By the end of that afternoon Elena could look back at the three boundaries from chapter three — wiggly, smooth, staircase — pick the one whose validation error was lowest, and report its test error as a number that meant what it said. The pushback she had heard from "Clinician A" — *"but on a different group of patients?"* — was no longer a hypothesis hanging in the air. She had the answer. And she had it because she had been willing, on day one, to set some data aside and not spend it.

## Versione didattica (objectives recap)

Gli obiettivi formali del capitolo vivono in [objectives.md](objectives.md). In sintesi, il payoff narrativo li ricalca: **diagnose** l'overfitting come *low training error + high error on new data* (il modello cattura idiosincrasie del campione, non il segnale); **apply** lo split *train / validation / test* assegnando a ciascun set un ruolo non sostituibile (fit / choose / report) e applicando la regola di contaminazione (*ogni decisione presa su un numero spende quel numero*); **use** $K$-fold cross-validation come procedura canonica per il validation step (divide in $K$ blocchi, train su $K-1$ + estimate su $1$, ripeti, media; $K \in [5, 10]$ standard).

## Pre-hook → 05 Deep Learning

> Elena now has a discipline that works for *any* classifier she can build — fit it, cross-validate it, hold out a test set, report the honest number. But what if the **model itself** can be made arbitrarily complex — a network of millions of artificial *neurons* that can, in principle, fit *anything* she shows it? The cross-validation discipline still holds. But the *architecture* of the model becomes a new question: how many layers, how many neurons, how do they connect? That class of models — **deep neural networks** — is next.
