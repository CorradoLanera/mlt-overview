100 words (3 acts)

The first deep network on Elena's screen carried millions of weights, and every paper she read called it a "black box". She braced herself: this time she would have to obey. Then she opened it. Each neuron was a logistic regression in disguise — a sigmoid swapped for any non-linear, differentiable function. Stacks of those, layer on layer, made a "deep" network. Training was gravity: the weights were coordinates, the loss was the height, and gradient descent walked the model downhill. By dusk she had drawn a network on a napkin and counted its weights. Nothing magic, just a bigger box.

Chapter arc — Hook → Challenge → Resolution → Payoff

Punto Funzione Contenuto
Hook aggancio The cross-validation discipline of chapter 4 has armed Elena to interrogate any classifier — but the first deep network she sees has somewhere between thousands and millions of weights, and the literature unanimously calls it a black box. For a moment she expects to have to take its outputs on faith.
Challenge / Data problema concreto How do you read a model with a million parameters? Is "deep" a name for some new kind of computation she hasn't seen before — or is it the same machinery, scaled up? Three questions in one: (i) what does one neuron actually compute? (ii) what happens when you connect many of them into layers? (iii) how does the network learn — i.e. how are the millions of weights chosen?
Resolution come si affronta One neuron is a generalised non-linear logistic regression: given a vector of inputs a=(a1,…,an)a = (a_1, \ldots, a_n) and weights w=(w1,…,wn)w = (w_1, \ldots, w_n), it computes out=g(∑iaiwi)\text{out} = g(\sum_{i} a_i \, w_i), where g:ℝ→ℝg:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R} is any non-linear and differentiable activation function (ReLU, tanh, sigmoid). A fully-connected network is just neurons stacked in layers: a[l]=g[l](W[l]a[l−1])a^{[l]} = g^{[l]}(W^{[l]} \, a^{[l-1]}). Learning is gradient descent on a loss landscape: each combination of weights WW assigns a single loss value L(W)L(W) — the height on a high-dimensional surface — and the update W←W−η∇WL(W)W \leftarrow W - \eta \, \nabla_W L(W) walks the model downhill. The two conditions on gg have operational meaning: non-linearity prevents the stack from collapsing to one big linear map (without it, no number of layers would add capacity); differentiability ensures the gradient ∇WL\nabla_W L exists, so the descent step has a direction at all.
Payoff cosa resta A deep network is not a new kind of computation; it is a composition of objects Elena already knew (logistic units), trained by a mechanism she can write down (gradient descent). A toy 3→2→2→13 \to 2 \to 2 \to 1 network has 1212 weights — she counts them on a napkin and writes its forward pass in four equations. The opacity of a real network is the opacity of scale, not of kind: a million weights instead of twelve is a quantitative jump, not a categorical one. The black box is bigger than the boxes of chapters 2–4, but it is no more sealed.

Narrative version (prose)

The screen showed a deep neural network — the kind every textbook calls a black box, with somewhere between thousands and millions of weights — and for a moment Elena felt she was back at chapter one, about to obey a verdict whose reasoning she could not read. The cross-validation discipline she had built in chapter four would still hold: she would test it before she trusted it. But how do you even describe a model with a million parameters? Could she look inside, the way she had looked inside a decision boundary?

She started small. One neuron. Forget the brain analogy; the equation was a familiar one. Given a vector of inputs a=(a1,…,an)a = (a_1, \ldots, a_n) and a vector of weights w=(w1,…,wn)w = (w_1, \ldots, w_n), a neuron computed out=g(∑iaiwi)\text{out} = g(\sum_{i} a_i \, w_i). That was a logistic regression with the sigmoid swapped out for any non-linear, differentiable function gg — the ReLUs and tanhs the literature kept naming. Nothing she had not met before. The neuron of "deep learning" was the first classifier of chapter two, with one mathematical freedom added: the shape of gg.

The deep part was composition: stack the units in layers, feed the outputs of one layer as the inputs to the next, formally a[l]=g[l](W[l]a[l−1])a^{[l]} = g^{[l]}(W^{[l]} \, a^{[l-1]}) for l=1,2,…,Ll = 1, 2, \ldots, L. A toy network with 33 inputs, two hidden layers of 22 neurons each, and one sigmoid output — call it 3→2→2→13 \to 2 \to 2 \to 1 — had exactly 3⋅2+2⋅2+2⋅1=123 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 1 = 12 weights. Twelve. She could draw it on a napkin. The forward pass was four equations long. A network with a million weights was the same idea, with 10510^5 more rows.

How did the thing learn? Each combination of weights WW assigned the model an error L(W)L(W) on its training data: the weights were coordinates in a high-dimensional space, and the error was a height. Picture a landscape — endless in dimension, but real enough. Random initial weights placed the model somewhere on a hillside. Gradient descent — W←W−η∇WL(W)W \leftarrow W - \eta \, \nabla_W L(W) — walked it downhill, one small step at a time, repeatedly. The network "learned" because it kept finding lower ground.

That was when the two conditions on gg — non-linear and differentiable — clicked into place. Non-linearity, because if every gg were linear the whole stack would collapse to one large linear map (no stacking would buy any new capacity, and twelve weights or twelve million would describe the same family of functions). Differentiability, because without a gradient at WW there is no direction in which to step, and the procedure stops being a procedure at all.

By the end of that afternoon the deep network was no longer a black box. It was a big one — with parts whose names she could now say: neuron, activation gg, layer, weights WW, loss L(W)L(W), gradient descent. The opacity people kept invoking was the opacity of scale, not the opacity of mystery. A million weights was a quantitative jump from twelve, not a categorical one. Elena did not yet know which patient features a particular network would pay attention to, or which it would ignore — that question was the next one, on her list for the morning. But she had stopped expecting magic. The atoms had been familiar all along.

Pre-hook → 06 Unstructured data (CNN/RNN)

Elena's stack of neurons can chew on any vector of inputs — age, BMI, a lab value — and learn from it. But the data she actually sees in clinic does not arrive as a tidy vector of length 1212. A chest X-ray is an image of 1024×10241024 \times 1024 pixels: feed it raw to a fully-connected layer and the first weight matrix alone would hold over a million parameters per neuron. A blood-pressure trace is a sequence, varying in length: where do you even start? The same logistic-stack-plus-descent machinery can survive the jump — but only with a clever rule about which weights are shared. That rule is the subject of the next chapter: convolutional networks for images, recurrent networks for sequences — the same atoms, organised to respect the structure of the data.
