## Six words

> **Stack logistic units; descent finds weights.**

*Glossa:* scelto via `narratore-critico` su 5 varianti. Mantiene la struttura a due clausole con punto e virgola della serie (01 *"Programs follow rules; learners find them."*, 02 *"Draw the line; count the cost."*, 03 *"Same data; three algorithms, three boundaries."*, 04 *"Decisions cost data; hold the test."*) — la coerenza tipografica è ormai cifra della serie e va rispettata. **"Stack logistic units"** è il primo dei due beat del capitolo: il neurone *è* una logistica generalizzata (eco esplicito a ch. 02 dove la logistica era già stata vista come *classificatore*), e *stack* è l'unico ingrediente "deep" — niente magia, solo composizione. **"Descent finds weights"** è il secondo beat: gradient descent come meccanismo unico di apprendimento, *finds* per insistere che i pesi *non* vengono progettati ma trovati (gem pedagogico contro la lettura "AI = programma di regole"). Lessico-perno **weights** seminato come gancio a ch. 06: gli stessi pesi diventeranno *kernel/filter* nelle convoluzionali (weight sharing è proprio la modifica strutturale che ch. 06 introduce).

## 100 words (3 acts)

The first deep network on Elena's screen carried millions of weights, and every paper she read called it a "black box". She braced herself: this time she would have to obey. Then she opened it. Each neuron was a logistic regression in disguise — a sigmoid swapped for any non-linear, differentiable function. Stacks of those, layer on layer, made a "deep" network. Training was gravity: the weights were coordinates, the loss was the height, and gradient descent walked the model downhill. By dusk she had drawn a network on a napkin and counted its weights. Nothing magic, just a bigger box.

*Struttura:* Bing = Elena affronta la deep network: milioni di pesi, "black box", l'aspettativa di doverla *subire* | Bang = la apre. Un neurone *è* una logistica con $g$ qualsiasi al posto della sigmoide; impilarli *è* il "deep"; l'addestramento *è* la discesa di un gradiente su un paesaggio di perdita | Bongo = a fine pomeriggio ha disegnato la rete su un tovagliolo e ha *contato* i suoi pesi. La scatola non era *chiusa*, era solo *più grande*. (100 parole esatte — la chiusura *"nothing magic, just a bigger box"* è il payoff narrativo: l'opacità del deep è di *scala*, non di *natura*.)

| **Hook** | aggancio | The cross-validation discipline of chapter 4 has armed Elena to interrogate *any* classifier — but the first deep network she sees has somewhere between thousands and millions of weights, and the literature unanimously calls it a *black box*. For a moment she expects to have to take its outputs on faith. |
| **Challenge / Data** | problema concreto | How do you *read* a model with a million parameters? Is "deep" a name for some new kind of computation she hasn't seen before — or is it the same machinery, scaled up? Three questions in one: (i) what does *one neuron* actually compute? (ii) what happens when you connect many of them into *layers*? (iii) how does the network *learn* — i.e. how are the millions of weights chosen? |
| **Resolution** | come si affronta | One neuron is a *generalised non-linear logistic regression*: given a vector of inputs $a = (a_1, \ldots, a_n)$ and weights $w = (w_1, \ldots, w_n)$, it computes $\text{out} = g(\sum_{i} a_i \, w_i)$, where $g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is any **non-linear** and **differentiable** activation function (ReLU, tanh, sigmoid). A *fully-connected* network is just neurons stacked in layers: $a^{[l]} = g^{[l]}(W^{[l]} \, a^{[l-1]})$. Learning is gradient descent on a loss landscape: each combination of weights $W$ assigns a single loss value $L(W)$ — *the height* on a high-dimensional surface — and the update $W \leftarrow W - \eta \, \nabla_W L(W)$ walks the model downhill. The two conditions on $g$ have operational meaning: *non-linearity* prevents the stack from collapsing to one big linear map (without it, no number of layers would add capacity); *differentiability* ensures the gradient $\nabla_W L$ exists, so the descent step has a direction at all. |
| **Payoff** | cosa resta | A deep network is **not** a *new* kind of computation; it is a *composition* of objects Elena already knew (logistic units), trained by a *mechanism* she can write down (gradient descent). A toy $3 \to 2 \to 2 \to 1$ network has $12$ weights — she counts them on a napkin and writes its forward pass in four equations. The opacity of a *real* network is the opacity of **scale**, not of *kind*: a million weights instead of twelve is a quantitative jump, not a categorical one. The black box is *bigger* than the boxes of chapters 2–4, but it is no more *sealed*. |

### Narrative version (prose)

The screen showed a deep neural network — the kind every textbook calls a *black box*, with somewhere between thousands and millions of weights — and for a moment Elena felt she was back at chapter one, about to obey a verdict whose reasoning she could not read. The cross-validation discipline she had built in chapter four would still hold: she would test it before she trusted it. But how do you even *describe* a model with a million parameters? Could she look inside, the way she had looked inside a decision boundary?

She started small. One neuron. Forget the brain analogy; the equation was a familiar one. Given a vector of inputs $a = (a_1, \ldots, a_n)$ and a vector of weights $w = (w_1, \ldots, w_n)$, a neuron computed $\text{out} = g(\sum_{i} a_i \, w_i)$. That was a logistic regression with the sigmoid swapped out for *any* non-linear, differentiable function $g$ — the ReLUs and tanhs the literature kept naming. Nothing she had not met before. The neuron of "deep learning" was the *first classifier* of chapter two, with one mathematical freedom added: the shape of $g$.

The *deep* part was composition: stack the units in layers, feed the outputs of one layer as the inputs to the next, formally $a^{[l]} = g^{[l]}(W^{[l]} \, a^{[l-1]})$ for $l = 1, 2, \ldots, L$. A toy network with $3$ inputs, two hidden layers of $2$ neurons each, and one sigmoid output — call it $3 \to 2 \to 2 \to 1$ — had exactly $3 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 1 = 12$ weights. *Twelve.* She could draw it on a napkin. The forward pass was four equations long. A network with a million weights was *the same idea*, with $10^5$ more rows.

How did the thing *learn*? Each combination of weights $W$ assigned the model an error $L(W)$ on its training data: the weights were coordinates in a high-dimensional space, and the error was a *height*. Picture a landscape — endless in dimension, but real enough. Random initial weights placed the model somewhere on a hillside. Gradient descent — $W \leftarrow W - \eta \, \nabla_W L(W)$ — walked it downhill, one small step at a time, repeatedly. The network "learned" because it kept finding lower ground.

That was when the two conditions on $g$ — *non-linear* and *differentiable* — clicked into place. *Non-linearity*, because if every $g$ were linear the whole stack would collapse to one large linear map (no stacking would buy any new capacity, and twelve weights or twelve million would describe the same family of functions). *Differentiability*, because without a gradient at $W$ there is no direction in which to step, and the procedure stops being a procedure at all.

By the end of that afternoon the deep network was no longer a black box. It was a *big* one — with parts whose names she could now say: *neuron*, *activation* $g$, *layer*, *weights* $W$, *loss* $L(W)$, *gradient descent*. The opacity people kept invoking was the opacity of *scale*, not the opacity of *mystery*. A million weights was a quantitative jump from twelve, not a categorical one. Elena did not yet know which patient features a particular network would pay attention to, or which it would ignore — that question was the next one, on her list for the morning. But she had stopped expecting magic. The atoms had been familiar all along.

## Versione didattica (objectives recap)

Gli obiettivi formali del capitolo vivono in [objectives.md](objectives.md). In sintesi, il payoff narrativo li ricalca: **describe** il neurone come *generalised non-linear logistic unit* $\text{out} = g(\sum_{i} a_i \, w_i)$ con $g$ non-lineare e differenziabile (una logistica con $g$ qualsiasi al posto della sigmoide); **compose** il forward pass di una piccola rete fully-connected ($a^{[l]} = g^{[l]}(W^{[l]} a^{[l-1]})$, conta i pesi) — l'esempio canonico è $3 \to 2 \to 2 \to 1$ con $12$ pesi; **explain** come la rete *impara*: pesi $W$ come coordinate di un paesaggio, loss $L(W)$ come altezza, gradient descent $W \leftarrow W - \eta \, \nabla_W L(W)$ come *cammino in discesa* — e ruolo operativo dei due vincoli su $g$ (non-linearità → lo stack non collassa a una mappa lineare; differenziabilità → il gradiente esiste e la direzione di discesa è ben definita).

## Pre-hook → 06 Unstructured data (CNN/RNN)

> Elena's stack of neurons can chew on any *vector* of inputs — age, BMI, a lab value — and learn from it. But the data she actually sees in clinic does not arrive as a tidy vector of length $12$. A chest X-ray is an image of $1024 \times 1024$ pixels: feed it raw to a fully-connected layer and the first weight matrix alone would hold over a million parameters *per neuron*. A blood-pressure trace is a sequence, varying in length: where do you even start? The same logistic-stack-plus-descent machinery can survive the jump — but only with a clever rule about *which weights are shared*. That rule is the subject of the next chapter: **convolutional** networks for images, **recurrent** networks for sequences — the same atoms, organised to respect the structure of the data.

*Glossa:* il pre-hook **non ritira** nulla di ciò che ch. 05 ha appena costruito (neurone come logistica generalizzata, stack, discesa) — al contrario, lo riafferma esplicitamente *"the same logistic-stack-plus-descent machinery can survive the jump"* — e introduce il **nuovo vincolo strutturale**: i pesi non possono essere *tutti diversi*. Cliff-hanger lessicale **convolutional / recurrent / weight sharing** (il vocabolario di ch. 06) senza ancora rivelare il meccanismo (kernel, stride, time-step). Il problem framing è dato in forma di *due dati clinici concreti* (RX torace + traccia di pressione arteriosa), così la motivazione "perché non basta la fully-connected" è clinica prima ancora che matematica — Elena ha già visto la matematica, e adesso la *misura del problema reale* la costringe a stringere il modello.
