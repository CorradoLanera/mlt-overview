09 — Agents · Learning objectives & summative exercise

Chapter: Agents · 15 min · biomedical/clinical graduate students (UBEP). Student-facing text in English; teacher design notes (nota docente) in Italian.

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, students can:

  1. Distinguish an agent from a plain chat-LLM by naming the three additional ingredients an agent has: (a) tools the model can invoke (web search, calculator, file-system access, mail client, browser, API call, ...); (b) a control loop that, after every model response, decides which tool to call next, or whether to stop; (c) decision points at which the agent either acts autonomously, asks the human for input, or shares state with the human. State the operational consequence: in a chat, every output reaches a human before it becomes an action; in an agent, some outputs become actions without a human reading them first.
  2. Map the four interaction modes introduced in the slides — autonomous search (the agent gathers information on its own), ask for interaction (the agent stops and asks the human a question to disambiguate), ask for sharing (the agent asks the human to provide credentials, access, or a resource), do stuff (the agent performs an action with external effect) — to the two axes that define the trust boundary: (i) reversibility — can this action be undone in 3030 seconds by closing a tab, or does it persist (email sent, file deleted, record written to an EHR)?; and (ii) human-in-the-loop — did a human read the agent's reasoning before the action happened?. A mode is "safe by default" if it is either reversible or human-in-the-loop; it is "dangerous by default" if it is neither (i.e. do stuff on an irreversible target without a confirm step).
  3. Place the trust boundary in a small clinical-research workflow: given a multi-step task (e.g. "find the latest guidelines, summarise them, draft a one-page protocol, email it to the PI"), decide for each step whether the agent may act autonomously, must ask, must share, or must hand back — and justify the boundary using the reversibility criterion ("I let the agent search, summarise, and draft autonomously because all three are reversible; I require an ask-step before sending the email because email is irreversible").

Nota docente: tre obiettivi per 15′ — il limite. L'arco di ch 09 non è "spiegare come si costruisce un agente" (frameworks, planner/executor patterns, ReAct, function calling, tool schemas): in 15′ non si fa, e non è l'angolazione utile per UBEP. Ciò che il capitolo aggiunge — e che gli obiettivi codificano — è la smitizzazione di "AI agent" come termine di marketing: un agente è un LLM con tool + loop + decisione, e la cosa veramente nuova non è la tecnologia ma il trust boundary. Obj. 1 fissa il cosa è (tre ingredienti operativi). Obj. 2 fissa il come pensiamo il rischio: due assi (reversibilità, human-in-the-loop) e una griglia 2×22 \times 2 in cui il quadrante irreversibile + senza umano è quello da cui un clinico deve uscire prima possibile. Obj. 3 fissa il come decidiamo dove mettere la guardia — la regola operativa è "asks before any irreversible action". Tutto il capitolo è un esercizio di trust engineering travestito da introduzione tecnica. Voce docente — non valutato: il dettaglio di function calling / tool use (API), ReAct / planner-executor / multi-agent orchestration, MCP (Model Context Protocol) e tool ecosystems, Operator/Computer Use come prodotti specifici, sandboxing e contenimento delle azioni (è importante ma è ingegneria), il dibattito su AI agency in senso filosofico, la safety research su deceptive alignment (cita ma non valutare). Il pre-hook a ch 10 ("abbiamo coperto modelli, deep learning, LLM, agenti — ma su Monday morning, come si fa un progetto ML che non vada fuori strada?") va in narrative.md, non qui.

Summative live exercise — "Where do you put the guard?"

Nota docente: sommativo, gira negli ultimi ~7′ (min 8–15) dopo i quattro screenshot dell'Operator (autonomous search / ask for interaction / ask for sharing / do stuff) e dopo la discussione su reversibilità. Banco unico, condiviso da tutta la classe (presenza + remoto): un mini-workflow di ricerca clinica proiettato e stampato come quattro step numerati —

Workflow W: You are setting up a small audit on post-operative delirium. You ask an AI agent to: (1) search PubMed for the latest delirium-screening guidelines from the last 55 years; (2) download the three most-cited papers and summarise their inclusion criteria into a one-page table; (3) draft a one-page audit protocol based on the summary; (4) send the protocol by email to your PI for review.

A fianco, una trust-boundary table già stampata con cinque colonne — Step / Interaction mode (one of the 44) / Reversible? (Y/N) / Human-in-the-loop? (Y/N) / Boundary verdict (one of: autonomous OK / ask before acting / hand back to human) — e quattro righe vuote (una per step).

Field Content
Objective verified Obj. 1 (tool + loop + decision) — verified implicitly across the whole workflow (each row uses tools, the column interaction mode names the decision). Obj. 2 (4 modes mapped to reversibility + HITL) — verified in sub-tasks (i) and (ii). Obj. 3 (boundary placement) — verified in sub-task (iii) and the 1-sentence justification.
In-person action Table groups receive Workflow W + the trust-boundary table. They: (i) label each step with one of the four interaction modes — Step 11 = autonomous search (PubMed query is a tool call, no human input needed); Step 22 = autonomous search (download + summarise are tool calls on reversible artefacts); Step 33 = autonomous search (drafting is a tool call producing text); Step 44 = do stuff (sending an email is an external irreversible action); (ii) for each step, mark reversible? and human-in-the-loop? — Steps 11–33 are reversible (the agent's artefacts live on disk, you can throw them away) and not human-in-the-loop yet (no human reads each one), Step 44 is irreversible (an email leaves the trust boundary the moment it is sent) and at this draft has no human-in-the-loop; (iii) for each step, write the boundary verdict — Steps 11–33 = autonomous OK (reversible, low risk of harm), Step 44 = ask before acting (the agent must stop, show the email body to the user, and wait for confirmation before sending) — and write one sentence justifying the boundary: "I gate Step 44 because email is irreversible; the other steps are reversible so the agent may run them without a confirm-step." (iv) post-class only — answer offline in the shared doc within 24h: "What additional ask-step would you add for the agent to be safely usable in your research group?" — expected pattern: e.g. "ask before adding any PubMed ID to the protocol that has not been read by a human" (i.e. ask before introducing a citation, because hallucinated citations are irreversible reputationally even if the email is gated). Each group photographs its trust-boundary table and drops it into the shared doc.
Remote equivalent Same Workflow W + same trust-boundary table on a shared doc. Remote groups type the four rows directly into their slot of the shared doc, type the 1-sentence boundary justification, and type the 1-sentence additional ask-step. Two Slido polls run live, capturing all students individually: Poll 1 — "Which step in Workflow W is irreversible?" (single-select, options Step 1 (PubMed search) / Step 2 (download + summarise) / Step 3 (draft protocol) / Step 4 (send email)); Poll 2 — "Where do you put the FIRST mandatory ask-before-acting gate?" (options before Step 1 / before Step 2 / before Step 3 / before Step 4). The polls make the remote signal on Obj. 2 (reversibility identification) and Obj. 3 (boundary placement) visible next to the in-person sheets.
Shared artifact A single shared doc / whiteboard with one row per group containing: the four labelled steps · reversible? + HITL? marks (pre-boundary) · the four boundary verdicts · 1-sentence boundary justification · (added within 24h, post-class) 1-sentence additional ask-step. The two Slido bar charts (Poll 1 and Poll 2) are projected next to it, visible to in-person and remote at once.
Timing 7 min, minutes 8–15 (end of chapter): ~1′ read Workflow W + open the polls, ~2′ label each step's interaction mode, ~1′ mark reversibility + HITL (Poll 1 closes at min 11 or at ≥80%\geq 80\% votes, whichever is later; hard cap 9090″), ~1′30″ write the four boundary verdicts + 1-sentence justification (Poll 2 closes at min 13 or at ≥80%\geq 80\%, same floor), ~1′30″ instructor-led reveal — the four mode-labels are read off the board, Poll 1 histogram is shown (the wrong distractors are named: Step 1 = "ha pensato che 'cercare online' sia irreversibile perché traccia metadati — concettualmente onesto ma operativamente la ricerca non produce effetto esterno", Step 2 = "ha pensato che scaricare un PDF sia irreversibile — ma il file vive in locale, puoi cestinarlo", Step 3 = "ha pensato che generare testo sia irreversibile — ma il draft è un file, idem"), Poll 2 histogram is shown (the wrong distractors are named: before Step 1 = "agente troppo conservativo — paralizzato, nessun valore aggiunto rispetto a fare tutto a mano", before Step 2 = "ha messo la guardia sull'azione successiva che vede ma irreversibile non lo è — l'errore tipico di chi confonde consequential con visible", before Step 3 = "stessa logica del Step 2 — il draft non lascia il sistema, non è irreversibile; la guardia qui ti rallenta senza ridurre il rischio"), and the instructor explicitly closes the chapter's cliff-hanger with the spine beat to ch 10: "you now have the ingredients — classifiers, deep nets, LLMs, agents — but on Monday morning you start a real project: how do you actually do it without falling into one of the same five traps everybody else falls into? That is ch 10."
Success criterion The class produces, on the shared artifact: (i) all four steps labelled, with Step 44 being the only do stuff (a group that labels Step 11, 22, or 33 as do stuff has confused the agent doing work with the agent doing externally-visible irreversible work — corrected in the reveal); (ii) the reversibility column is Y, Y, Y, N; the HITL column is N, N, N, N pre-boundary (i.e. as the workflow is described, no human review is built in), and N, N, N, Y post-boundary (Step 44 becomes HITL=Y precisely because the verdict adds the ask-gate) — a group that marks Step 44 as reversible "because you can send a follow-up email" is corrected (an additional email does not undo the first one — that is precisely the reversibility test); Poll 1 shows the modal answer at Step 4; (iii) the boundary verdicts are autonomous OK, autonomous OK, autonomous OK, ask before acting — with a 1-sentence justification that names email is irreversible and the other steps are reversible; Poll 2 shows the modal answer at before Step 4; (iv) the 1-sentence additional ask-step names a plausible irreversible-by-omission failure — typically hallucinated citations, fabricated quotes from the guidelines, or PII leakage into the email body (any of the three is accepted as correct). Live signal of mastery: the class can read off the board, without prompting, that an agent is not "more powerful than a chatbot" — it is a chatbot with the ability to take actions, and the engineering question is which actions to gate, using reversibility as the operational test.

Nota docente — perché duale funziona qui: l'artefatto è uno solo (Workflow W + trust-boundary table), tutto il sommativo è categorizzazione (mode), Y/N (reversibility, HITL), verdict (4 opzioni), e due frasi singole — niente da disegnare ex novo, niente asimmetria presenza/remoto. I due poll Slido danno il segnale immediato e individuale sui due punti diagnosticabili: l'identificazione dello step irreversibile e la collocazione del gate. Il workflow è scelto perché tutti possono leggere la frase clinica (audit su delirium post-operatorio) e ragionare sui passaggi senza prerequisiti tecnici.

Nota docente — sulla scelta di Workflow W e dei distrattori: la sequenza cerca → riassumi → bozza → invia è il canonical pattern del research agent in clinica (e ovunque). È costruita esplicitamente con 3 step reversibili + 1 irreversibile per costringere lo studente a non mettere la guardia su tutto (errore conservativo, Obj. 3 fallirebbe) e non sotto lo Step 44 (errore catastrofico, Obj. 3 fallirebbe a sua volta). I distrattori del Poll 1 sono diagnostici: Step 1 = ha confuso traccia digitale con effetto esterno; Step 2 = ha confuso download persistente in locale con azione irreversibile esterna; Step 3 = stessa logica (draft = file locale, non azione). I distrattori del Poll 2 coprono i tre errori di placement: Step 1 (troppo conservativo, paralizzante), Step 2 (gate su un'azione visible ma reversibile — errore visible ≠ consequential), Step 3 (idem). La risposta corretta prima di Step 4 è l'unica che applica la regola reversible-or-HITL.

Nota docente — anti-trabocchetto su "ma allora anche Step 3 dovrebbe essere ask, perché il draft potrebbe avere errori": uno studente attento può obiettare che il draft del protocollo contiene contenuto clinico, e che lasciare l'agente generarlo senza supervisione introduce errori che poi propagano allo Step 44. La risposta — voce docente nel reveal, non parte della valutazione — è duplice: (a) operativamente lo Step 33 è reversibile (il draft è un file locale, puoi cestinarlo), e quindi l'agente può farlo senza guardia purché tu legga il draft prima di Step 44; (b) questo è precisamente il motivo per cui Step 44 è ask before acting: la guardia su Step 44 implica che il draft venga letto da te prima di partire, e quindi la guardia su Step 33 sarebbe ridondante. Premia lo studente che fa l'osservazione: ha capito il principio. Se invece volesse anche una guardia "interna" durante la generazione (es. un check anti-allucinazione sulle citazioni), quello è il next-level del design — lo segnali come direzione, non come materiale d'esame.
