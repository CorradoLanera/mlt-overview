> **Set size note:** chapter 9 closes at **4 items**, matching [chapter 6](../06-unstructured-data/items_valutativi.md). The choice is deliberate: cap. 9 has **three objectives** that each warrant *operational* testing (Obj. 1 = distinguish, Obj. 2 = map to the $2 \times 2$ grid, Obj. 3 = place the gates), **plus a natural synthesis target** — the chapter's rule (*ask before any irreversible action*) can be subtly mis-applied in an integrated design problem, and that misapplication is precisely what a clinician will encounter in the wild (a colleague proposing an agent with gates in the wrong place). Three items would test the three objectives *separately*; a fourth item is needed to test whether the student can detect the rule's *application failure* in a design they did not write — which is the operational form of Obj. 1 + Obj. 2 + Obj. 3 together. The chapter's $15'$ budget is tight, but the conceptual surface (3 ingredients + 4 interaction modes + 2 axes + placement discipline) is broader than the *practice*-only chapters (cap. 5, cap. 7, cap. 8 — all $3$ items each) and the *single mechanism* chapter (cap. 6 — $4$ items because it introduces twin parallel mechanisms CNN+RNN around weight sharing). Cap. 9 introduces a *new dimension of trust engineering*, not just a new mechanism, and the synthesis item is the chapter's *Round-8 closure* — the test that says *can you spot a mis-gated agent in the wild?*. Without it, the item bank measures *the rule's three components separately* but not *the rule's integrated use*.

- Knowledge: $0$
- Comprehension: $1$ (item 01)
- Application: $2$ (items 02 + 03)
- Argumentation/evaluation: $1$ (item 04)

Cap. 9 is **multi-categorial**, unlike cap. 8 (mono-categorial Application). The reason is the chapter's structure: cap. 9 introduces a *definition* (Obj. 1, naturally tested at Comprehension level — *do you recognise an agent when you see one?*), a *test* (Obj. 2, naturally tested at Application level — *can you apply the $2 \times 2$ grid?*), a *discipline* (Obj. 3, naturally tested at Application level — *can you place the gates?*), and the *full operational use* of the rule on someone else's design (synthesis, naturally tested at Argumentation level — *can you detect a mis-application?*). Each objective sits at its natural cognitive level; flattening them all to Application (as in cap. 8) would *lose* the discrimination between *recognising the kind of object* and *engineering with it*. The chapter teaches a *new dimension* of trust engineering, and the item bank registers that dimension at four cognitive levels.

## Internal coherence — the four items together test the chapter's *trust boundary discipline*

The chapter's narrative closes on Elena's notebook sentence ([narrative.md](narrative.md), penultimate paragraph): *"An agent is not smarter than a chat-LLM; it is a chat-LLM that can act. The engineering question is which actions to gate. Reversibility is the test. The gate goes exactly where reversibility fails."* The four items map onto the four operational moves required to *enact* that sentence:

- **Item 01** tests *which actions to gate at all* — i.e. *which systems are agents* (chat-LLMs with the ability to act) as opposed to chats, autocompletes, or scripted automations. A student who cannot tell the difference does not yet know *what* they are gating; the rule has no target. Item 01 measures this *upstream* of the rule's application.
- **Item 02** tests *whether the action is dangerous-by-default* — i.e. the operational $2 \times 2$ test on a single workflow. The student picks the dangerous-quadrant step from four candidates, three of which reproduce specific named confusions (sensitivity, visibility, no-HITL-alone). Item 02 measures the *test* of the rule.
- **Item 03** tests *placing the gates* — i.e. the operational discipline on a workflow with *two* irreversible steps. A student who has internalised Item 02's test on a single step now has to scale it: classify *every* step, gate *exactly* the dangerous ones, *not* the reversible ones, *justify each*. Item 03 measures the *application* of the rule at workflow scale.
- **Item 04** tests *spotting a mis-application in the wild* — the operational *meta-skill* the chapter is ultimately for. A colleague has *almost* applied the rule; the student must see the substitution (destructiveness for reversibility), the operational consequence (over-gated step 3, under-gated steps 4 and 5), and the corrected placement. Item 04 measures the *generalisation* of the rule beyond the student's own designs.

Together, the four items verify the chapter's *discipline* rather than its *vocabulary*. A student who passes all four has demonstrated: *what an agent is* (item 01), *what to test* (item 02), *how to test it on their own workflow* (item 03), and *how to test it on someone else's* (item 04). The chapter's summative exercise — *Where do you put the guard?* on Workflow W — is recalled in **none of the four items**, by design: the items measure the *rule*, not the *summative's specific scenario*.

## Anti-leakage — vocabulary boundary with cap. 10

The items stay strictly within cap. 9 vocabulary: *agent*, *chat-LLM*, *tools*, *control loop*, *decision points*, *four interaction modes* (*autonomous search*, *ask for interaction*, *ask for sharing*, *do stuff*), *reversibility* + the $\sim 30$-second test, *human-in-the-loop* (HITL), *trust boundary*, *$2 \times 2$ grid*, *safe-by-default* and *dangerous-by-default*, *gate* and *placement*, the operational rule *ask before any irreversible action*, and the *over-gating* / *under-gating* failure modes (the latter is named in cap. 8's pre-hook as *paralyzingly conservative*, hence legitimately within the chapter's lexicon).
