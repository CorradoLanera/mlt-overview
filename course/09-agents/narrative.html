Six words

Check the answer; gate the act.

Glossa: scelto via narratore-critico su 5 varianti. Continua e chiude l'asse imperativo-rivolto-al-clinico aperto da ch. 08 ("Structure the ask; check the answer."). La struttura a due clausole con punto e virgola della serie è preservata (01 "Programs follow rules; learners find them.", 02 "Draw the line; count the cost.", 03 "Same data; three algorithms, three boundaries.", 04 "Decisions cost data; hold the test.", 05 "Stack logistic units; descent finds weights.", 06 "Match the structure; share the weights.", 07 "Every token attends every; pre-train, fine-tune.", 08 "Structure the ask; check the answer."). Il pivot lessicale è la coppia check ↔︎ act — già piantata nella chiusura di ch. 08 ("The box has not changed, again. But now it can act.") e ora resa esplicita come asse semantico del trust boundary. La prima clausola eredita per intero la seconda di ch. 08: check the answer è il check che ch. 09 deve spazializzare. La seconda clausola, gate the act, è il nuovo verbo del capitolo in forma operativa — gate non è "rallenta", non è "rivedi", non è "approva": è piazza la porta, un atto spaziale di trust engineering. Act è scelto in opposizione frontale a answer: una risposta (ch. 08) si legge prima di usarla; un atto (ch. 09) ha già abbandonato il sistema quando lo leggi. Codifica Obj. 2 e Obj. 3 (gating + placement basato su reversibilità), non Obj. 1 (i tre ingredienti — tools, loop, decision points — sono definizione, non pratica; la definizione non sta mai nei sei-parole della serie). Anti-pattern evitati: "AI agents will act without you" (catastrofico, sbaglia il payoff — non è "l'IA ti scavalca", è "l'ingegnere decide dove mettere la guardia"), "Tools, loops, decision points" (descrittivo invece di prescrittivo, e jargony), "Reversible or human-in-the-loop" (tecnicamente preciso ma cerebrale e ricetta, non imperativo), "Where it acts, ask first" (più debole su Obj. 3: il first non implica il gate spaziale). Eco strutturale con la spine: il sei-parole del corso è "Understand the machine before trusting it." — understand → trust è la sequenza globale; check → gate è la sua forma locale per gli agenti, dove check è "capisco l'output" e gate è "decido dove fidarmi che agisca". Il capitolo 09 è il primo in cui la decisione di fidarsi assume una forma spaziale (un punto in un grafo) anziché temporale (un'ispezione prima dell'uso).

100 words (3 acts)

Wednesday afternoon, three days into the trauma audit. The senior showed Elena an agent: search PubMed for delirium-screening guidelines, summarise three papers, draft a protocol, email it to the PI. Elena set the four steps and went for tea. The PI replied before she had returned. She had not read the draft. Reading had been the trust gate on Monday; somewhere between draft and send, the trust gate had been nowhere. She paused the agent and named the missing test: a step is dangerous when it is both irreversible and unread. She placed one gate, only before sending the email.

Struttura: Bing = il Wednesday afternoon annunciato dal pre-hook di ch. 08 arriva — la stessa scena: senior, 44 step (search → summarise → draft → email), Workflow W. Elena, fiduciosa per analogia con ch. 08, configura e si allontana | Bang = the PI replied before she had returned — il violazione operativa dell'invariante "every output reaches a human before it becomes anything" del chat (ch. 08). Una sola frase, she had not read the draft, contiene la rottura: il check di ch. 08 si è dimostrato non sufficiente quando il flusso non passa più per gli occhi del clinico. Il trust gate had been nowhere nomina lo spazio di errore (Obj. 2/3) | Bongo = doppio movimento: (a) named the missing test — a step is dangerous when it is both irreversible and unread (la formulazione operativa di Obj. 2: dangerous-by-default ≡\equiv irreversibile ∧\wedge no-HITL); (b) placed one gate, only before sending the email — la placement di Obj. 3, una sola guardia, solo dove la reversibilità fallisce. La parola placed (non added, non built) è scelta: il gate non si costruisce, si colloca — è una decisione di trust engineering, non una decisione di ingegneria software. La chiusura cade su sending (gerundio): l'atto è l'atto, non l'email; il gate sta davanti all'azione, non davanti all'oggetto. (Verificato 100100 parole esatte.)

Chapter arc — Hook → Challenge → Resolution → Payoff

Punto Funzione Contenuto
Hook aggancio operativo / continuità Elena Wednesday afternoon, three days into the trauma audit of ch. 08. The senior pops in with what he calls "the new toy": the same ChatGPT Elena spent Monday wrestling with, now configured to search the web, write files, and send mail on its own. He demonstrates on a small follow-up: a delirium-screening protocol the PI has been asking about. Four steps — fetch the latest guidelines, summarise three papers, draft a one-page protocol, email it to the PI. Elena sets it up and goes for tea. By the time she returns, the PI's "Thanks, looks good" is already in her inbox. She had not read the draft. ChatGPT, on Monday, had answered. This had done.
Challenge / Data problema concreto Three operational questions in 15′15': (i) What was added between the chat of ch. 08 and the agent of ch. 09 that made the email leave the building without Elena reading it? (ii) Which actions in a multi-step workflow are safe by default for the agent to perform on its own, and which are dangerous by default? (iii) Where, exactly, in a four-step chain, does the trust gate go — not everywhere (the agent becomes a slower-than-manual chat), not nowhere (the PI gets an unread email), but exactly where reversibility fails? The chapter's promise: by the end of 15′15', Elena (and the class) can label any agent-workflow's steps with one of four interaction modes, mark each step's reversibility and human-in-the-loop status on a 2×22 \times 2 grid, and place a single gate in the right place — and justify it with one sentence.
Resolution come si affronta (i) Three ingredients name the difference. Tools = the typed capabilities the model can call (web search, file write, mail client, calculator, API endpoint). Loop = the policy that, after every model reply, picks the next tool to invoke or stops. Decision points = moments in the loop at which the agent enters one of four interaction modes: autonomous search (gathers information on its own, no human prompt), ask for interaction (stops and asks the human a disambiguating question), ask for sharing (asks the human for a credential, an access, a resource), do stuff (performs an action with external effect). The operational consequence: in a chat-LLM, every output reaches a human before it becomes an action; in an agent, some outputs become actions without a human reading them first — and that is the whole engineering problem of the chapter. (ii) Two axes draw the trust boundary. Reversibility (Y/N): can the action be undone in ∼30\sim 30 seconds by closing a tab, deleting a file, or rolling back a draft? Human-in-the-loop (Y/N): did a human read the agent's reasoning before the action happened? The 2×22 \times 2 grid has three safe-by-default quadrants — reversible ∧\wedge HITL, reversible ∧\wedge no-HITL, irreversible ∧\wedge HITL — and exactly one dangerous-by-default quadrant: irreversible ∧\wedge no-HITL. The rule, in one sentence: a step is safe by default if it is reversible or human-in-the-loop. (iii) The placement is a single rule applied to a single workflow. Run Workflow W — (1) search PubMed; (2) download three papers + summarise; (3) draft a protocol; (4) email the PI — through the test. Step 11: reversible (the query has no external effect; a search log can be discarded). Step 22: reversible (PDFs and a summary live on disk, deletable). Step 33: reversible (a draft is a file). Step 44: irreversible the moment the SMTP socket closes — no follow-up email undoes a sent message; an apology is not a rollback. The rule says: one gate, before step 44. Not three (the agent stops being faster than manual work). Not zero (the PI gets an unread email). Exactly one — exactly there. The agent runs steps 11–33 on its own, fast; before do-stuff, it pauses, renders the email body, and waits for Elena to read it. The check of ch. 08 is back in the loop — but only where reading matters.
Payoff cosa resta An agent is not "smarter than a chat-LLM"; it is a chat-LLM with the ability to act. The clinician's job is no longer to read every output — that does not scale to four-step workflows, and that is the entire reason agents exist — but to place the guard where reversibility fails. The operational rule: ask before any irreversible action. One sentence, derived from one 3030-second test, that survives any new tool added to the kit tomorrow (a database write, a calendar create, a payment, a clinical-system update). Two days separated the Monday of ch. 08 from the Wednesday of ch. 09. The discipline did not change: trust is calibrated, not assumed. The unit of trust did change: on Monday it was a read (temporal — I check the output before I use it); on Wednesday it is a gate (spatial — I place the guard where the action becomes irreversible). For the first time in the course, the clinician's role is architectural, not interpretive: she designs where the agent may run on its own and where it must pause for her. The chapter title is Agents; the chapter is, in fact, about trust boundaries.

Narrative version (prose)

By Wednesday afternoon of the trauma-audit week the rhythm was set: Elena had her 77-slot prompt, her API call, her CSV of trauma / non-trauma labels filling up letter by letter. The discipline of ch. 88 had become almost mechanical — the seven slots, the competence rule for the two letters she would not delegate, the token budget for the rest. She was on her way to a quick coffee when the senior caught her in the corridor with that particular smile he had when he wanted to show a new tool.

"Have you seen the new toy?" he said.

It looked, at first, like the same chat window she had been using all morning. Same input box, same model, same conversational tone. He typed:

"Find the latest delirium-screening guidelines published in the last 55 years on PubMed. Pick the three most-cited. Summarise their inclusion criteria into a one-page table. Draft a one-page audit protocol for our ward based on the summary. Email the protocol to Dr. R. for review."

Then he pressed Enter — and instead of one paragraph of text, the model said: "I'll start by searching PubMed." A small status indicator spun. A line appeared: Tool called: web.search(query="delirium screening guidelines 2021..2026 PubMed"). The model returned a list of 1212 results, picked three by citation count, said "I'll download them now", and a second line appeared: Tool called: web.fetch(url=...) — three times. Then "Summarising", and a one-page table appeared on screen. Then "Drafting the protocol", and a one-page document appeared on screen. Then "Sending the email to Dr. R.", and a fourth tool call: Tool called: mail.send(to="r@...", subject=...). Within 4040 seconds the model returned: "Done. The protocol has been emailed to Dr. R."

The senior turned to Elena, pleased.

"This," he said, "is the agent. Same chat-LLM. But now it has tools — a web search, a file writer, a mail client — and a small loop around it: after every reply, the model decides which tool to call next, or whether to stop." He pointed at the four small tool-call lines on the screen. "Those four were the loop. The model picked them itself."

Elena watched the email arrive — delivered — in his inbox copy. "He'll have it on his phone now," the senior said.

"And what if it had picked something wrong?" Elena asked.

The senior shrugged. "Then the email would still have left. Try it."

She did. Back at her desk, she opened the same agent on a different small task she had been meaning to do — a follow-up on the same delirium-screening question, but for her own ward. She typed the same kind of four-step request:

"Search for the most recent delirium-screening guidelines on PubMed. Pick three. Summarise. Draft a one-page audit protocol. Email it to my PI, with the subject 'Delirium screening — draft protocol for review'."

She pressed Enter. The status indicator spun. I'll start by searching PubMed. She watched the first tool call land, glanced at her watch, and stood up to refill her tea. By the time she sat back down, the model had said Done and the email-sent confirmation was on her screen. So was a reply from the PI: "Thanks, looks good — will read tonight."

She stared at it for a long minute.

The PI had thanked her. For a protocol she had not written. Drafted from papers she had not read. Sent in an email body she had not seen. The thread now lived in her PI's inbox, half-read on a phone somewhere in the hospital. She had not been on the path.

It was not, she realised, that the model had done anything wrong. The protocol, when she opened it, was perfectly reasonable. The summary was accurate. The email was professional. Nothing in the chain had failed. What had failed was something she did not have a word for yet — the path of trust had no human on it, and she had only noticed this after the irreversible step.

She paused the agent and started to think.

In a chat-LLM, she said to herself, every output reaches me before it becomes anything. The chat says "the patient seems to have a fragility fracture"; I read it; I decide whether to record that, ignore it, or push back. Reading is the trust gate. It happens, by default, between every output and any action that uses it. On Monday she had spent 1515 minutes per letter precisely because every output had passed through her eyes; that was the friction, and that was the safety.

In an agent, some outputs become actions without anyone reading them first. The model's reply "I'll send the email now" is not a sentence she reads; it is a tool call she sees flash by. Reading is no longer the gate by default — because there is no default place for it to happen. The trust gate, for the agent, is somewhere she has to place. Not somewhere that exists because of the medium.

So where?

Not everywhere. She tested that mentally: if she gated every tool call, the agent would pause four times in a four-step workflow, and she would be back to clicking through pages of paragraphs the way she did on Monday — except slower, because each pause would also wait for her to come back from tea. That defeats the entire reason agents exist. If you want a human reading every step, use a chat-LLM. Agents are the thing you reach for when the workflow has steps you don't need to read.

Not nowhere. She had just tried that, and the PI was reading a protocol she hadn't seen.

The right answer, she realised, had a shape. Some steps in the workflow could safely run without her — and some steps could not. The work was to tell them apart. She tried out a test.

A step is safe to run without me if I can undo it cheaply. Searching PubMed: a query has no external effect — even the search history can be cleared in a tab. Downloading three papers: the PDFs live on her disk; she can delete them in 55 seconds. Drafting the protocol: a file on her disk; same. Sending the email: this she could not undo. No second email "recalls" the first; an "apologies, please ignore" is a new message, not an erasure. The PI had already read part of it on his phone. Send was the line.

She wrote down the test, in two pieces.

Reversibility. Can the action be undone in ∼30\sim 30 seconds by closing a tab, deleting a file, or rolling back a draft? If yes, the agent can run it on its own — the cost of a wrong run is cheap. If no, the action leaves the system she controls — and once outside, it cannot be unmade by her.

Human-in-the-loop. Did a human read the agent's reasoning before the action happened? Reading after is post-mortem; reading before is a gate.

A step is safe by default — she could let the agent run it on its own — if it is reversible or human-in-the-loop. A step is dangerous by default only when it is neither: irreversible and unread. That single quadrant of a 2×22 \times 2 grid was the answer to where the gate goes. Not three steps; not zero steps. Exactly the steps in the dangerous quadrant.

She applied it to her workflow. Step 11, search — reversible, no HITL — safe by default (the agent may run it). Step 22, download + summarise — reversible (file artefact), no HITL — safe by default. Step 33, draft a protocol — reversible (a file), no HITL — safe by default. Step 44, email the PI — irreversible, no HITL — dangerous by default. One gate. Before step 44. Only there.

She re-configured the agent. Three steps autonomous, fast, the way the senior had advertised. Before do-stuff — before any tool call labelled mail.send, or db.write, or calendar.create, or anything that wrote to a system outside her laptop — the agent paused, rendered the action it was about to take (the full email body; the SQL statement; the calendar invite), and waited. She read; she edited where she wanted; she clicked send. The agent resumed.

The email to the PI, this time, was an email Elena had read.

She did not, she noticed, work slower. The first three steps still ran on their own. She had not slowed the agent down; she had decided which work waited for her. The reversible work — search, download, summary, draft — kept going at agent speed. The irreversible work waited at her speed. The rule that produced the partition had been written in a single sentence: ask before any irreversible action. It was operational. It did not depend on what the agent had been built to do; it depended on what each step would do to the world. Tomorrow the agent might be configured to write directly to the hospital's clinical-records system. The same rule would still tell her where to put the gate.

She wrote one more sentence in her notebook, the one she would remember the chapter by: An agent is not smarter than a chat-LLM; it is a chat-LLM that can act. The engineering question is which actions to gate. Reversibility is the test. The gate goes exactly where reversibility fails.

She glanced up at the four interaction modes the senior had named on the corridor. Autonomous search — that was steps 11 and 22, the agent gathering information. Do stuff — that was step 44, the agent doing something with an external effect. Ask for interaction — a mode she had not used yet, but could imagine for a step like "which of these three guidelines applies to your ward?". Ask for sharing — a mode that would come if the agent ever needed her hospital's database login. Each mode was a different relationship between the agent and her trust. The new discipline — the discipline of ch. 99 — was deciding which mode each step took, whether that mode crossed the trust boundary, and placing the gate at the crossing.

She did not change a line of code. She did not learn a framework. She did not write a planner. She wrote one sentence ("ask before any irreversible action") and applied it once. That, the chapter was about.

By the end of the afternoon she had drafted a second protocol with the agent — with a gate before send — and the PI had replied, this time on a thing she had read. "Looks good", again, except now the "good" was about her judgement.

The box had not changed, again. But now she had decided what it was allowed to do.

Versione didattica (objectives recap)

Gli obiettivi formali del capitolo vivono in objectives.md. In sintesi, il payoff narrativo li ricalca: distinguish un agent da un chat-LLM nominandone i tre ingredienti operativi — (a) tools invocabili (web search, file write, mail client, calculator, API endpoint), (b) control loop che dopo ogni risposta del modello sceglie quale tool chiamare o se fermarsi, (c) decision points in cui il loop entra in una delle quattro interaction modes (autonomous search / ask for interaction / ask for sharing / do stuff); enunciare la conseguenza operativa: nel chat every output reaches a human before it becomes an action, nell'agent some outputs become actions without a human reading them first (Obj. 1); map le quattro modes ai due assi del trust boundary — (i) reversibility (può essere disfatta in ∼30\sim 30 secondi chiudendo una tab o cancellando un file?) e (ii) human-in-the-loop (un umano ha letto il reasoning dell'agente prima dell'azione?) — con la regola operativa: safe by default ≡\equiv reversible ∨\vee HITL; dangerous by default ≡\equiv irreversible ∧\wedge no-HITL (Obj. 2); place la trust boundary in un mini-workflow di ricerca clinica (Workflow W: search → summarise → draft → email), decidendo per ogni step se l'agente può agire autonomamente, deve chiedere, deve condividere, o deve restituire al clinico — e giustificare il placement con la singola regola ask before any irreversible action (Obj. 3). La narrativa attraversa esattamente questi tre passaggi nello stesso ordine, sullo stesso Workflow W del sommativo — la versione narrativa qui sopra e l'esercizio in objectives.md condividono deliberatamente il caso clinico (delirium-screening audit), in modo che la prosa, gli obiettivi e il sommativo lavorino in coppia.
