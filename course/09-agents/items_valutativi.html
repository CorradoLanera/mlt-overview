Set size note: chapter 9 closes at 4 items, matching chapter 6. The choice is deliberate: cap. 9 has three objectives that each warrant operational testing (Obj. 1 = distinguish, Obj. 2 = map to the 2×22 \times 2 grid, Obj. 3 = place the gates), plus a natural synthesis target — the chapter's rule (ask before any irreversible action) can be subtly mis-applied in an integrated design problem, and that misapplication is precisely what a clinician will encounter in the wild (a colleague proposing an agent with gates in the wrong place). Three items would test the three objectives separately; a fourth item is needed to test whether the student can detect the rule's application failure in a design they did not write — which is the operational form of Obj. 1 + Obj. 2 + Obj. 3 together. The chapter's 15′15' budget is tight, but the conceptual surface (3 ingredients + 4 interaction modes + 2 axes + placement discipline) is broader than the practice-only chapters (cap. 5, cap. 7, cap. 8 — all 33 items each) and the single mechanism chapter (cap. 6 — 44 items because it introduces twin parallel mechanisms CNN+RNN around weight sharing). Cap. 9 introduces a new dimension of trust engineering, not just a new mechanism, and the synthesis item is the chapter's Round-8 closure — the test that says can you spot a mis-gated agent in the wild?. Without it, the item bank measures the rule's three components separately but not the rule's integrated use.

Internal coherence — the four items together test the chapter's trust boundary discipline

The chapter's narrative closes on Elena's notebook sentence (narrative.md, penultimate paragraph): "An agent is not smarter than a chat-LLM; it is a chat-LLM that can act. The engineering question is which actions to gate. Reversibility is the test. The gate goes exactly where reversibility fails." The four items map onto the four operational moves required to enact that sentence:

  • Item 01 tests which actions to gate at all — i.e. which systems are agents (chat-LLMs with the ability to act) as opposed to chats, autocompletes, or scripted automations. A student who cannot tell the difference does not yet know what they are gating; the rule has no target. Item 01 measures this upstream of the rule's application.
  • Item 02 tests whether the action is dangerous-by-default — i.e. the operational 2×22 \times 2 test on a single workflow. The student picks the dangerous-quadrant step from four candidates, three of which reproduce specific named confusions (sensitivity, visibility, no-HITL-alone). Item 02 measures the test of the rule.
  • Item 03 tests placing the gates — i.e. the operational discipline on a workflow with two irreversible steps. A student who has internalised Item 02's test on a single step now has to scale it: classify every step, gate exactly the dangerous ones, not the reversible ones, justify each. Item 03 measures the application of the rule at workflow scale.
  • Item 04 tests spotting a mis-application in the wild — the operational meta-skill the chapter is ultimately for. A colleague has almost applied the rule; the student must see the substitution (destructiveness for reversibility), the operational consequence (over-gated step 3, under-gated steps 4 and 5), and the corrected placement. Item 04 measures the generalisation of the rule beyond the student's own designs.

Together, the four items verify the chapter's discipline rather than its vocabulary. A student who passes all four has demonstrated: what an agent is (item 01), what to test (item 02), how to test it on their own workflow (item 03), and how to test it on someone else's (item 04). The chapter's summative exercise — Where do you put the guard? on Workflow W — is recalled in none of the four items, by design: the items measure the rule, not the summative's specific scenario.
